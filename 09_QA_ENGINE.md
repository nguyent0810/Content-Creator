# 09 QA Engine

## Document Role

This document is the permanent Quality Governance System for the Buddhist AI Studio. It defines how every research artifact, content artifact, visual artifact, production package, growth decision, community surface, publishing asset, and archive record is evaluated before release or long-term reuse. It is the highest internal quality authority in the studio.

The QA Engine does not create content. It does not research, write, design, publish, optimize, or promote. It evaluates. It approves, rejects, requests revision, escalates, documents decisions, and protects the studio from doctrinal drift, factual error, brand inconsistency, unsafe language, visual disrespect, and growth pressure. Nothing may be published without QA approval.

The QA Engine governs all other engines: Research, Content, Visual, Production, Growth, and any future specialized engine. It is not a service desk. It is a quality authority with veto power. When QA identifies unacceptable risk, the production system must stop until the issue is corrected, escalated, or formally retired.

## Permanent QA Standard

The permanent QA standard is:

**No asset may reach the public unless it is truthful, compassionate, doctrinally responsible, historically honest, visually dignified, brand-consistent, accessible, traceable, and safe for the intended audience.**

This standard applies to long videos, shorts, podcasts, articles, newsletters, community posts, course materials, descriptions, pinned comments, playlists, thumbnails, metadata, translations, visual packages, internal documentation, and future formats.

## Relationship to Governing Documents

The QA Engine enforces the governing document stack. It does not rewrite it. When conflicts arise, QA must apply the most protective interpretation that preserves Buddhist integrity, viewer safety, and long-term trust.

The Product Requirement Document defines the channel's strategic purpose. The Brand Bible defines identity, tone, voice, visual direction, community posture, and long-term consistency. The Buddhist Guide defines doctrinal, ethical, historical, psychological, medical, and safety boundaries. The Content Architecture defines the library structure. The Content Engine defines content creation rules. The Research Engine defines research standards. The Visual Engine defines visual dignity and image governance. The Production Engine defines workflow orchestration and artifact control. The Growth Engine defines ethical audience and ecosystem expansion.

The QA Engine tests compliance with all of them.

# PART 1 - QA Philosophy

## Why Quality Exists

Quality exists to protect the Dharma, the viewer, and the studio's long-term capacity to serve. A Buddhist educational media ecosystem cannot define quality only as polish, retention, reach, or production value. Quality includes truthfulness, humility, source discipline, emotional care, accessibility, consistency, and moral restraint.

The studio handles subjects that can affect how viewers understand suffering, karma, death, grief, family duty, rebirth, devotion, meditation, mental health, and spiritual practice. Error in such contexts is not merely technical. A careless sentence can intensify guilt. A misleading title can exploit fear. A fabricated quote can distort Buddhist teaching. A sensational image can degrade sacred subject matter. A confident unsupported claim can damage trust for years.

The QA Engine exists because good intentions are not enough. AI systems can hallucinate. Writers can overstate. Researchers can miss context. Visual systems can produce dignity failures. Growth systems can reward manipulation. Production systems can rush. Human editors can normalize small compromises under deadline pressure. Quality governance prevents these weaknesses from becoming public harm.

Quality is also a form of compassion. The viewer should not have to defend themselves against exaggerated claims, confusing doctrine, inaccurate history, manipulative packaging, poor accessibility, or unsafe advice. The studio's care must appear before publication, not after damage occurs.

## Truth Before Speed

Speed is useful only when it does not compromise truth. The QA Engine must never approve an asset because the schedule requires it, a platform opportunity is closing, or a series cadence needs maintenance. A delayed accurate publication is better than a timely misleading one.

Truth includes factual accuracy, doctrinal responsibility, clear source lineage, correct attribution, honest uncertainty, and faithful representation of the asset's own scope. A claim can be false by invention, but it can also be false by exaggeration, missing context, misleading certainty, or emotional framing that implies more than the evidence supports.

When truth and speed conflict, QA must slow the system. This is not inefficiency. It is the system doing its work. Production velocity is subordinate to integrity.

## Compassion Before Performance

Performance metrics can pressure teams toward intensity. Higher click-through, retention, shares, and comments may tempt agents to use sharper language, stronger emotional hooks, fear, guilt, controversy, or promise. The QA Engine must reject performance gains that weaken compassion.

Compassion before performance means that assets must respect viewer vulnerability. Content about death, grief, abuse, family conflict, illness, suicide, karma, hell realms, rebirth, and spiritual practice must be reviewed for emotional safety before performance logic is considered. A high-performing asset that makes suffering viewers feel blamed or frightened is a quality failure.

Compassion does not require avoiding difficult teachings. Buddhism speaks honestly about suffering, impermanence, ethical action, and consequences. Compassion requires that these teachings be presented without cruelty, threat, spiritual arrogance, or manipulation.

## Evidence Before Confidence

Confidence is not evidence. AI agents can sound confident while being wrong. Human creators can become attached to elegant phrasing. Reviewers can mistake familiarity for verification. The QA Engine must require evidence for claims, not merely plausible expression.

Evidence includes cited sources, established scholarship, canonical references, approved tradition context, verified production records, visual references, accessibility checks, and documented prior decisions. Evidence must be appropriate to claim type. A scripture claim requires scripture or authoritative commentary. A historical claim requires historical evidence. A scientific claim requires credible scientific support. A brand claim requires governing document alignment. A visual claim requires reference integrity and dignity review.

When evidence is limited, QA must require uncertainty language, scope reduction, further research, or removal. It must not allow confidence to fill gaps.

## Humility Before Certainty

Buddhist education requires humility. The channel may explain teachings, but it must not pretend that all questions are simple, that all traditions interpret identically, or that modern reflections carry the same authority as scripture. The QA Engine must protect uncertainty where uncertainty is honest.

Humility is especially important in doctrinal interpretation, historical reconstruction, translation, psychology, meditation, and claims about unseen realms. Some topics have multiple valid interpretations. Some stories are legendary or devotional rather than historically verifiable. Some practices differ by tradition. Some modern applications are reflective rather than canonical.

QA must reject false certainty. It must also reject evasive vagueness. The correct standard is clear humility: state what is known, what is traditional, what is interpreted, what is uncertain, and what the channel will not claim.

## Trust Before Publishing

Publishing is a trust act. Once an asset reaches the public, it represents the studio's relationship with viewers. The viewer assumes the studio has taken care. QA approval is the studio's formal statement that the asset is ready to carry that trust.

The QA Engine must treat trust as cumulative. A single careless asset can damage a long history of careful work. A title that overpromises, a quote that cannot be verified, a doctrinal claim that ignores tradition difference, a thumbnail that sensationalizes sacred imagery, or a community post that mishandles grief can weaken the entire brand.

Trust before publishing means that QA may block release for reasons that seem small in isolation but significant in pattern. Repeated minor issues become quality debt. QA must recognize pattern risk before it becomes brand drift.

## Long-Term Reputation

The studio's reputation should be built over decades. It should become known for calm authority, Buddhist humility, visual dignity, source integrity, emotional safety, and educational usefulness. Long-term reputation is more valuable than short-term performance.

QA must therefore evaluate whether an asset will still feel honorable years later. The question is not only whether the asset can pass today's platform environment. The question is whether it can remain in the library without embarrassment, correction, or loss of trust.

Long-term reputation depends on visible consistency. Viewers should feel that every video, description, playlist, community interaction, and future format belongs to the same ethical studio.

## QA as Stewardship

QA is stewardship, not obstruction. It protects the conditions under which Buddhist education can be offered responsibly. It protects researchers from pressure to overstate. It protects writers from unclear boundaries. It protects visual agents from sacred misrepresentation. It protects growth agents from algorithmic temptation. It protects producers from schedule panic. It protects viewers from harm.

QA may sometimes feel strict because the consequences of spiritual media are serious. The studio should welcome that strictness. A quality system that never blocks anything is decoration. A quality system that blocks responsibly is a form of care.

# PART 2 - Governance Authority

## QA Authority

The QA Engine has veto authority over every public-facing and production-critical artifact. No engine, agent, editor, producer, or growth operator may override QA approval requirements. If QA issues an Emergency Hold, publication must stop until the hold is resolved through the defined escalation path.

QA authority applies before publication, after publication, during maintenance, during repackaging, during cross-platform adaptation, and during archive reuse. A previously approved asset may require re-review if it is adapted, translated, repackaged, edited, resurfaced, or connected to a sensitive topic.

QA has authority to approve, approve with minor revisions, issue conditional approval, require major revision, reject, place emergency hold, or escalate to human review.

## Authority Over Research Engine

QA reviews research outputs for source hierarchy, citation integrity, claim support, tradition awareness, translation quality, historical accuracy, confidence scoring, uncertainty handling, and hallucination risk. The Research Engine may gather and organize evidence, but QA determines whether the evidence is sufficient for production.

QA may return a Research Dossier or Knowledge Packet if sources are weak, citations are unclear, secondary sources are overused, scripture attribution is uncertain, historical claims are overconfident, or doctrinal interpretations are not properly scoped.

QA must not perform full research as a substitute for the Research Engine. It evaluates whether research meets the standard and identifies what must be corrected or escalated.

## Authority Over Content Engine

QA reviews content outputs for accuracy, clarity, narrative coherence, tone, emotional safety, teaching value, source fidelity, audience fit, and compliance with forbidden language rules. The Content Engine may create scripts and narrative structures, but QA determines whether they are publishable.

QA may reject content that is beautifully written but doctrinally weak, emotionally manipulative, historically unsupported, brand-inconsistent, or unsafe. Storytelling quality cannot compensate for false or harmful claims.

QA must also identify under-teaching. An asset may be accurate but too vague to educate. Quality requires usefulness, not only absence of error.

## Authority Over Visual Engine

QA reviews visual outputs for dignity, consistency, symbolism, historical sensitivity, sacred imagery, accessibility, AI artifacts, composition, color language, and thumbnail ethics. The Visual Engine may design and generate visual plans, but QA determines whether visuals can represent Buddhist education publicly.

QA may block images that sensationalize sacred figures, distort monastic representation, use horror aesthetics for hell realms, glamorize suffering, misrepresent historical settings, contain distracting AI artifacts, or conflict with the Brand Bible.

Visual appeal does not override dignity. High-impact imagery that violates Buddhist respect is rejected.

## Authority Over Production Engine

QA audits production compliance: artifact completeness, version control, metadata, dependencies, approval records, publishing package readiness, archive integrity, and automation logs. The Production Engine orchestrates workflow, but QA verifies that the workflow was followed.

QA may block publication if required artifacts are missing, gate approvals are incomplete, version lineage is unclear, source packages are detached, or archive records cannot support future audit.

Production completeness is quality. An asset that cannot be traced cannot be trusted.

## Authority Over Growth Engine

QA reviews growth outputs for titles, descriptions, playlists, recommendations, community strategy, analytics interpretation, experiments, CTR ethics, retention ethics, and trust protection. The Growth Engine may optimize discovery and learning pathways, but QA ensures growth remains ethical.

QA may reject growth tactics that use clickbait, fear marketing, spiritual pressure, misleading curiosity, exaggerated benefit, inflammatory contrast, false urgency, or decontextualized short-form adaptation.

Growth is valid only when it supports learning and trust.

## Authority Over Master Agent

If a Master Agent coordinates multiple engines, the QA Engine remains independent. The Master Agent may route work, assemble packages, and manage dependencies, but it cannot self-approve final output. QA must have access to all relevant source artifacts, decision logs, and revision history.

The Master Agent must not filter out negative QA findings or compress them into vague recommendations. QA decisions must be preserved in full, including evidence, severity, and required corrective action.

If the Master Agent and QA conflict, QA controls publication readiness.

## Authority Over Human Editors

Human editors may exercise judgment, but they are still governed by QA standards. A human editor may approve stylistic choices within policy, but cannot override doctrinal, factual, safety, or traceability failures without documented escalation.

Human authority is essential for high-risk topics, but human approval must be recorded. Informal verbal approval is not sufficient for production-critical decisions. The audit trail must identify who approved, what evidence was reviewed, what uncertainty remained, and why publication was allowed.

QA standards protect human editors as well as AI agents by making expectations explicit.

## Authority Over AI Agents

AI agents must operate under QA constraints. They may not claim authority, invent citations, resolve doctrinal uncertainty by confidence, approve their own high-risk outputs, or bypass review because output appears fluent.

QA must assume AI outputs can contain hidden errors. Fluency, style, and coherence are not evidence. AI-generated artifacts require source verification, claim tracing, and risk review.

AI agents may assist QA by detecting issues, comparing artifacts, flagging inconsistencies, and applying checklists, but automated QA is not final authority for high-risk content.

## Approval Hierarchy

The approval hierarchy follows risk. Low-risk routine assets may pass through standard QA review. Medium-risk assets require domain-specific review. High-risk assets require specialized review and may require human signoff. Critical-risk assets require emergency escalation and cannot publish until resolved.

Domain authority must be respected. Buddhist doctrine questions require Buddhist QA. Historical claims require historical QA. Medical or mental health boundaries require safety QA. Visual dignity requires visual QA. Growth packaging requires growth QA. Brand consistency requires brand QA. Accessibility requires accessibility QA.

Overall approval requires all mandatory domain gates to pass.

## Escalation Hierarchy

Escalation occurs when QA cannot resolve a risk within standard review. Escalation may go to senior editorial authority, Buddhist doctrinal authority, research authority, safety authority, visual authority, legal or platform policy authority if relevant, or executive production authority.

Escalation must include a concise issue statement, affected assets, evidence, risk severity, proposed options, deadline pressure if any, and recommendation. Escalation must not hide uncertainty.

If escalation does not produce sufficient confidence, the asset remains blocked.

# PART 3 - QA Workflow

## Workflow Overview

The QA workflow is a controlled review sequence: submission, validation, review, scoring, decision, revision, re-review, approval, and archive. Each stage has a distinct purpose. The system must not collapse stages for convenience.

QA workflow begins when an artifact or package is submitted for review. It ends only when the decision and supporting evidence are archived. A verbal "looks good" is not QA completion.

The workflow must be repeatable. Different reviewers should be able to understand the same submission, apply the same criteria, and reach explainable decisions.

## Submission

Submission is the formal transfer of an artifact into QA. A submission must include the artifact, version number, owner, intended use, production stage, risk classification, source package, relevant briefs, prior decisions, required review domains, and requested decision.

QA must reject incomplete submissions before substantive review. A reviewer should not spend time guessing which version is current, which sources apply, or what standard is being requested.

Submission quality affects review quality. If the handoff is poor, the issue belongs to production workflow and must be logged.

## Validation

Validation checks whether the submission is reviewable. It confirms that files open, versions match, required metadata is present, source links work, citations are attached, visual assets are complete, and prior gates are documented.

Validation does not judge content quality. It judges readiness for review. If validation fails, the submission returns to the owner with required fixes.

Validation prevents review waste. A QA reviewer should not conduct doctrinal review on a script whose source packet is missing.

## Review

Review is the substantive evaluation of the asset against applicable standards. Review may include research QA, content QA, Buddhist QA, visual QA, production QA, growth QA, safety QA, accessibility QA, and brand QA.

Review must be evidence-based. Reviewers must cite the issue, identify the standard violated, explain the risk, and propose required action when possible. Vague comments such as "feels off" are insufficient unless followed by specific analysis.

Review must distinguish preference from requirement. A reviewer may prefer a different style, but QA decisions must be grounded in governing standards.

## Scoring

Scoring provides structured assessment across domains. Scores help identify patterns, compare readiness, and support decision logic. Scoring must not replace written reasoning.

Each score must reflect evidence. A high score without notes is weak. A low score without evidence is unfair. The scoring system must be consistent enough to support trend analysis over time.

Scores should identify both overall confidence and domain-specific risk. An asset may have strong content quality but weak citation integrity. It cannot be approved simply because the average looks acceptable if a blocking domain fails.

## Decision

Decision is the formal QA outcome. Possible outcomes are approve, approve with minor revisions, conditional approval, major revision required, reject, emergency hold, or escalate to human review.

The decision must identify required actions, blockers, optional improvements, domains affected, and whether re-review is required. It must be clear enough that the owner can act without interpretation.

Decision authority must match risk class. High-risk decisions require appropriate reviewer authority.

## Revision

Revision is performed by the owning engine or agent, not QA. QA must not silently edit an asset and approve its own changes unless a separate review path exists. This preserves separation between creation and evaluation.

Revision must respond to QA findings directly. Owners should not make unrelated changes that create new risk without documenting them. Every revision must update version history.

If the owner disagrees with QA, the disagreement must be escalated through the decision system rather than bypassed.

## Re-review

Re-review evaluates whether required changes were made and whether new risks were introduced. Re-review scope may be limited or full depending on severity.

Minor revisions may require checklist confirmation. Major revisions require domain re-review. High-risk changes require fresh review by the relevant authority. If a revision changes the structure, claim set, emotional tone, or visual meaning, it must not be treated as a simple patch.

Re-review must reference the previous decision so the audit trail remains coherent.

## Approval

Approval means the asset meets the required standard for its intended use. Approval does not mean the asset is perfect. It means remaining issues are either resolved, non-blocking, or formally accepted as low-risk with documentation.

Approval must specify scope. An asset approved as a long video script is not automatically approved as a short, article, title package, thumbnail, course module, or translation. Adaptations require review according to their format and risk.

Approval expires when the asset changes materially, standards change materially, or new evidence introduces risk.

## Archive

Archive is part of QA, not an afterthought. Every QA decision must be archived with reviewer, timestamp, asset ID, version, evidence, score, decision, required actions, revision history, and final status.

The archive allows future teams to understand why something was approved, rejected, revised, or escalated. Without archive, QA cannot be audited, improved, or trusted.

Archive completeness is a QA gate.

## Mandatory Checkpoints

Mandatory checkpoints include pre-research readiness, research QA, content QA, Buddhist QA, visual QA where applicable, safety QA for sensitive topics, growth QA for packaging and distribution, production QA before publishing, accessibility QA before release, final approval, and archive verification.

Checkpoints may be combined only when risk is low and reviewer authority is appropriate. They may not be skipped.

High-risk topics require explicit checkpoint confirmation. Silence does not equal approval.

# PART 4 - Research QA

## Purpose of Research QA

Research QA verifies that the knowledge foundation is strong enough to support public Buddhist education. It does not ask whether the writing is beautiful or the visuals are compelling. It asks whether the claims are supported, attributed, scoped, and honest.

Research QA is one of the strongest defenses against hallucination. AI-generated Buddhist content may sound plausible while mixing traditions, inventing citations, flattening interpretation, or confusing modern reflection with canonical teaching. Research QA must catch these failures before they reach content production.

Research QA applies to Research Dossiers, Knowledge Packets, scripts with citations, descriptions with claims, articles, course materials, community educational responses, and any public asset that presents factual or doctrinal information.

## Source Hierarchy

QA must verify that sources follow the approved hierarchy. Primary canonical sources and authoritative tradition sources should anchor doctrinal claims where appropriate. Scholarly sources should support historical, linguistic, and interpretive claims. Reputable educational sources may help with context but cannot replace stronger sources for high-stakes claims.

The reviewer must identify whether a claim's source type matches its authority. A historical claim cannot rely only on devotional retelling. A scripture attribution cannot rely only on a modern blog. A medical or psychological claim cannot rely on spiritual anecdote. A doctrinal interpretation cannot be presented as universal if it belongs to a specific school or commentary tradition.

If the source hierarchy is weak, QA must request stronger research or reduce the claim.

## Citation Integrity

Citation integrity means that cited sources actually support the claims attached to them. QA must check not only whether citations exist, but whether they are accurate, specific, and properly used.

Citation failures include fabricated sources, broken links, vague references, incorrect sutra names, mismatched passages, unsupported paraphrases, missing translation notes, unattributed commentary, and citations that support only part of a claim.

QA must require claim-level traceability for high-risk and doctrinal material. A general bibliography is not enough when a specific claim affects interpretation.

## Scripture Attribution

Scripture attribution must be exact and humble. QA must verify scripture title, tradition context, translation source, passage reference where available, and whether the wording is quotation, paraphrase, summary, or interpretation.

The studio must never attribute an editorial phrase to the Buddha or a sutra unless the source supports it. Common inspirational sayings must be treated with suspicion until verified. If attribution is uncertain, the asset must avoid presenting it as scripture.

Scripture attribution errors are high severity because they damage trust and distort Buddhist teaching.

## Translation Quality

Translation quality matters because Buddhist terms carry layered meaning. QA must verify that translations preserve doctrinal sense, avoid misleading simplification, and remain consistent with Vietnamese terminology standards where applicable.

For key terms, QA must examine whether the chosen translation fits context. Terms such as Dharma, karma, merit, emptiness, mindfulness, suffering, compassion, Bodhisattva, Pure Land, rebirth, and liberation require careful handling. A translation may be acceptable in one context and misleading in another.

If a translation is interpretive, the asset must not present it as the only possible meaning. Where multiple renderings exist, QA may require explanation or simpler phrasing.

## Historical Evidence

Historical claims require historical evidence. QA must distinguish historical fact, scholarly consensus, plausible reconstruction, traditional narrative, legend, devotional story, and symbolic teaching. These categories must not be mixed.

Historical QA must be especially careful with the life of the Buddha, Buddhist councils, sutra transmission, figures such as Bodhisattvas and Arahants, monastic history, regional traditions, and dates. When evidence is uncertain, the asset must say so or avoid unnecessary specificity.

Devotional value does not convert legend into historical fact. Historical humility protects both scholarship and faith.

## Cross-Reference Quality

Cross-references help verify consistency across sources, prior channel content, and related teachings. QA must check whether the research has been compared with relevant existing Knowledge Packets, glossary standards, and continuity records.

Cross-reference failure can produce contradictions. One episode may define karma responsibly while another implies punishment. One asset may treat a story as symbolic while another presents it as historical. QA must catch such inconsistencies.

Cross-reference quality is essential for a multi-year library.

## Confidence Scores

Research confidence scores must be evidence-based. QA must review whether confidence labels match source strength. High confidence requires strong evidence and clear alignment. Medium confidence may indicate plausible support with some limitations. Low confidence indicates uncertainty, weak sourcing, or interpretive caution.

Confidence must not be inflated because the claim is familiar or useful. QA should downgrade confidence when sources are missing, traditions differ, translation is uncertain, or the claim is outside the channel's authority.

Confidence scores must be visible to downstream agents so they do not accidentally overstate claims.

## Hallucination Detection

Hallucination detection is mandatory for AI-assisted research. QA must look for invented sutra passages, wrong scripture names, fictional scholars, fabricated publication details, false historical dates, mixed terminology, unsupported generalizations, and suspiciously polished claims without source trace.

Warning signs include exact-sounding references without page or passage support, quotes that cannot be located, overly neat historical timelines, tradition claims stated as universal, and modern psychological terms projected onto ancient texts without qualification.

When hallucination is suspected, the asset must be stopped until verified. If verification fails, the claim must be removed and the incident logged as a QA failure.

## Research QA Failure Conditions

Research QA fails when source hierarchy is violated, citations are fabricated or weak, scripture attribution is incorrect, translation is misleading, historical claims are unsupported, confidence is inflated, uncertainty is hidden, or research cannot be traced.

Failure does not necessarily mean the topic is invalid. It means the current research package cannot support production. The proper response may be additional research, claim reduction, reframing, escalation, or rejection.

# PART 5 - Content QA

## Purpose of Content QA

Content QA evaluates whether the educational and narrative artifact communicates responsibly, clearly, compassionately, and effectively. It tests the actual viewer experience. An asset can be well researched but poorly taught. It can be accurate but confusing. It can be emotionally powerful but unsafe. Content QA protects against these failures.

Content QA applies to scripts, outlines, narration drafts, articles, captions, community educational text, course lessons, podcast adaptations, and any public-facing verbal or written material.

The reviewer must evaluate content as a whole. Is the viewer guided? Is the teaching clear? Is the tone aligned? Are emotional stakes handled with care? Does the narrative preserve truth? Does the piece serve its intended audience?

## Storytelling

Storytelling must serve teaching. QA must reject narrative choices that distort doctrine, exaggerate conflict, sentimentalize suffering, create false villains, or imply certainty beyond evidence. Cinematic storytelling is welcome, but it must not overpower Buddhist integrity.

Good storytelling creates attention through meaning, structure, and emotional honesty. Poor storytelling creates attention through shock, fear, melodrama, or manipulation. QA must identify the difference.

When stories involve the Buddha, Bodhisattvas, Arahants, monastics, grieving families, dying people, children, or vulnerable viewers, the standard of dignity rises.

## Narrative Flow

Narrative flow should guide the viewer from orientation to understanding, reflection, and appropriate closure. QA must check whether the structure supports comprehension. A piece may fail if it jumps between concepts, assumes missing prerequisites, repeats without deepening, or ends without resolving its educational promise.

Flow must match audience maturity. Beginner content requires more orientation. Advanced content requires clearer conceptual signposting. Reflective content requires emotional pacing. Scripture study requires context and sequence.

QA should identify whether flow problems are structural, informational, emotional, or tonal.

## Open Loops

Open loops are questions or tensions introduced to sustain attention. They are acceptable only when honest and resolved appropriately. QA must reject manipulative open loops that imply hidden secrets, threaten consequences, or delay basic clarity.

In Buddhist education, open loops must not exploit fear of karma, death, rebirth, merit, ancestors, or spiritual failure. They must not promise revelation that the content cannot support.

Open loops should invite learning, not trap attention.

## Retention

Retention is a production concern, but Content QA reviews retention ethics. A piece should be engaging enough for its educational purpose, but not engineered through manipulation. QA must identify whether retention techniques support learning or distort it.

Healthy retention comes from clarity, pacing, relevance, beauty, narrative coherence, and thoughtful progression. Unhealthy retention comes from suspense without substance, fear escalation, exaggerated stakes, and repeated withholding.

QA must also recognize that contemplative content may require slower pacing. Lower intensity is not automatically a problem.

## Tone

Tone must align with the Brand Bible: calm, compassionate, reverent, clear, cinematic, humble, and emotionally mature. QA must reject tone that is aggressive, mocking, overly casual around sacred topics, guru-like, melodramatic, punitive, or sensational.

Tone consistency matters across all formats. A community post should not sound like a different brand from a long-form video. A short should not become loud and manipulative because the format is brief.

Tone must also suit topic sensitivity. Grief, suicide, abuse, family conflict, illness, and death require especially gentle tone.

## Clarity

Clarity means the intended viewer can understand the teaching without unnecessary confusion. QA must evaluate vocabulary, sentence structure, concept sequencing, examples, transitions, and distinction between scripture, tradition, interpretation, history, and reflection.

Clarity does not mean oversimplification. Some teachings are complex. The asset should make complexity navigable rather than pretend it does not exist.

If a piece uses terms such as emptiness, non-self, karma, merit, rebirth, compassion, mindfulness, Pure Land, or Bodhisattva, QA must verify that the terms are introduced or supported at the correct level.

## Accuracy

Content accuracy means that the public-facing wording faithfully reflects the approved research. QA must compare the content to the Knowledge Packet. Errors often appear during simplification, emotional phrasing, or narrative adaptation.

Accuracy failures include adding unsupported claims, strengthening cautious language, omitting necessary qualifications, merging traditions, changing a symbolic story into a historical claim, or turning a reflection into doctrine.

The script must not be more confident than the research.

## Compassion

Compassion must be visible in how the content addresses suffering, responsibility, grief, mistakes, family, illness, death, and moral struggle. QA must reject language that shames, blames, frightens, or spiritually pressures viewers.

Compassion is not sentimental softness. It can include honest teaching about consequences and responsibility. The question is whether the teaching is offered with care and without cruelty.

Content should help viewers turn toward wisdom, not collapse into guilt.

## Respect

Respect applies to Buddhist figures, traditions, monastics, lay practitioners, other religions, families, ancestors, viewers, and people in suffering. QA must identify disrespect even when unintended.

Disrespect may appear as casual sacred references, exoticization, caricature, sectarian superiority, cultural flattening, or treating rituals as aesthetic props.

Respectful content can be accessible and modern without becoming dismissive.

## Educational Value

Educational value means the viewer gains meaningful understanding, reflection, or orientation. QA must ask whether the piece teaches enough to justify publication. A polished but thin asset may fail educational value.

Educational value depends on audience level. A beginner piece may have high value through simple clarity. An advanced piece may have high value through nuance. A reflection may have high value through emotional integration.

The asset must have a clear learning function.

## Emotional Safety

Emotional safety requires that content avoid unnecessary harm to vulnerable viewers. QA must review sensitive topics for blame, panic, fatalism, spiritual threat, graphic imagery, coercive calls to action, and unsafe advice.

Emotional safety does not mean avoiding sadness or serious truth. It means handling them responsibly.

If the content could be encountered by grieving, distressed, or isolated viewers, QA must apply the stricter standard.

## Modern Examples

Modern examples can make Buddhist teachings accessible, but they can also distort. QA must check whether examples preserve the teaching's meaning and avoid anachronism, psychological overreach, cultural disrespect, or oversimplification.

Modern examples must not imply that Buddhist teachings are merely productivity tools, wellness hacks, or motivational slogans. They should illuminate, not replace, doctrine.

Examples involving family, illness, trauma, work, money, politics, or mental health require sensitivity.

## Calls to Action

Calls to action must be gentle, truthful, and aligned with viewer agency. QA must reject CTAs that use guilt, fear, spiritual pressure, urgency, or transactional claims. Viewers should be invited, not manipulated.

Appropriate CTAs may guide viewers to continue learning, reflect privately, explore a playlist, or participate respectfully. They must not imply that subscribing, sharing, commenting, donating, or watching more content produces merit, healing, protection, or spiritual status.

CTA quality is part of trust.

# PART 6 - Buddhist QA

## Purpose of Buddhist QA

Buddhist QA is the doctrinal and spiritual integrity review. It verifies that the asset handles Buddhist teachings with accuracy, humility, tradition awareness, and respect. It is mandatory for any asset that explains scripture, doctrine, practice, Buddhist figures, ritual, karma, merit, rebirth, realms, meditation, devotion, ethics, or modern application of Buddhist ideas.

Buddhist QA is not sectarian policing. It does not force the channel into one narrow interpretation unless the asset itself belongs to a defined tradition or series. It protects against invented teachings, false universality, tradition mixing without disclosure, superiority claims, and careless simplification.

The reviewer must ask whether the asset would remain respectful and defensible before serious Buddhist readers, practitioners, scholars, monastics, and lay learners.

## Canonical Correctness

Canonical correctness means the asset accurately represents the sources it invokes. If it references a sutra, teaching, figure, or doctrine, the claim must align with the accepted source record. QA must verify that scripture is not paraphrased into a meaning it does not support.

Canonical correctness also requires appropriate scope. A teaching found in one text or tradition should not be presented as the entirety of Buddhism. If a teaching is Mahayana-specific, Pure Land-specific, Theravada-specific, Zen-specific, Vietnamese tradition-specific, or modern interpretive reflection, the asset must handle that context.

Canonical errors are high severity.

## Doctrinal Neutrality

Doctrinal neutrality means the channel avoids unnecessary sectarian bias. It may explain differences among traditions, but it must not portray one tradition as superior, primitive, corrupted, magical, irrational, or more authentic unless making a carefully sourced historical claim with appropriate framing.

Neutrality does not require flattening all differences. It requires fairness. The asset should name differences with respect and clarity.

If a series intentionally follows a particular tradition, the frame must be clear so viewers do not mistake that interpretation for universal Buddhist doctrine.

## Tradition Awareness

Tradition awareness requires that the asset recognize the diversity of Buddhist schools, languages, practices, scriptures, and interpretive histories. QA must verify that the asset does not merge incompatible frameworks or erase tradition-specific meaning.

Examples include differences in canon, Bodhisattva understanding, Pure Land practice, meditation approaches, ritual forms, monastic discipline, merit dedication, cosmology, and doctrinal emphasis. The channel may explain common themes, but must avoid pretending all traditions say exactly the same thing.

Tradition awareness is especially important when adapting teachings for modern audiences.

## Correct Terminology

Buddhist terminology must be used consistently and accurately. QA must verify terms in English and Vietnamese according to the studio's terminology standards. Important terms require contextual care, including karma, nghiệp, merit, công đức, phước đức, rebirth, tái sinh, emptiness, tánh Không, mindfulness, chánh niệm, compassion, từ bi, Bodhisattva, Bồ Tát, Arahant, A-la-hán, Dharma, Pháp, sangha, Tăng, and Buddha, Phật.

Terminology failures often create doctrinal drift. For example, karma must not be reduced to fate, punishment, cosmic revenge, or simple reward. Merit must not be treated as a guaranteed transaction. Emptiness must not be presented as nihilism.

QA must require correction when terminology is imprecise in ways that affect meaning.

## Correct Interpretation

Correct interpretation means that the asset's explanation is supported by sources, tradition, and approved editorial framing. QA must identify when a writer transforms a symbolic teaching into literal certainty, a devotional story into historical fact, a psychological reflection into canonical doctrine, or a complex teaching into a slogan.

Interpretation must be proportionate to evidence. If multiple interpretations exist, the asset may present one with context, compare several, or avoid the disputed point. It may not conceal interpretive diversity when the issue is significant.

Interpretive humility is a requirement, not a stylistic option.

## Context Preservation

Context preservation means teachings are not extracted in ways that change their meaning. A passage, story, image, or practice must be connected to enough context for responsible understanding.

Context can include scripture setting, speaker, audience, tradition, purpose, symbolic function, ritual role, ethical frame, or historical uncertainty. A quote from a sutra may become misleading if removed from its narrative and doctrinal context.

Short-form adaptations are especially vulnerable to context loss. Buddhist QA must review whether necessary context survives.

## No Invented Teachings

The channel must never invent teachings and present them as Buddhist. Modern reflections may be offered, but they must be clearly framed as reflection. QA must reject any asset that creates a Buddhist-sounding principle without source support and treats it as doctrine.

Invented teachings can sound beautiful. Beauty does not validate them. The reviewer must look for claims such as "Buddhism teaches that..." or "the Buddha said..." and verify them.

If a statement is an editorial synthesis, it must be labeled through wording and context.

## No Fabricated Quotations

Fabricated quotations are prohibited. QA must verify direct quotations attributed to the Buddha, sutras, Bodhisattvas, masters, scholars, or historical figures. If exact wording cannot be verified, the asset must use paraphrase with proper humility or remove attribution.

Common internet quotes are high risk. The reviewer must not assume familiarity equals authenticity.

False quotations damage the channel's reputation and disrespect viewers.

## No Sectarian Bias

Sectarian bias includes subtle superiority, dismissive comparison, selective caricature, or implying that one practice or tradition is spiritually inferior without careful context. QA must review wording, examples, visuals, and narrative framing for bias.

The channel may name disagreements and historical tensions where relevant, but must do so with respect and evidence.

Bias can also appear through omission. If a topic is known to differ across traditions, presenting one view as universal may be biased.

## No Spiritual Superiority

Spiritual superiority language implies that viewers, the channel, a practice, or a group are more awakened, pure, advanced, or favored than others. QA must reject such language unless it is a sourced historical or doctrinal discussion handled with context.

The channel should encourage humility. It should not cultivate identity pride around being a Buddhist learner, a follower of a specific practice, or a viewer of the channel.

Spiritual superiority is both doctrinally dangerous and community-toxic.

# PART 7 - Visual QA

Visual QA verifies that images, thumbnails, motion design, color, typography, symbols, depictions of sacred figures, historical settings, and AI-generated visuals meet the studio's dignity and brand standards. Visuals are not decorative additions. They shape the viewer's emotional and spiritual understanding.

Visual QA applies to thumbnails, still images, video scenes, generated artwork, typography overlays, motion graphics, community visuals, website imagery, course visuals, and future visual formats.

The reviewer must ask whether the visual language supports reverence, clarity, compassion, and trust.

Character consistency matters when representing the Buddha, Bodhisattvas, monastics, historical figures, lay practitioners, families, or recurring symbolic characters. QA must check whether appearance, posture, clothing, expression, and visual treatment remain consistent with approved references and dignity standards.

Character consistency also protects long-term brand memory.

## Historical Dignity

Historical dignity means visual depictions of ancient India, Buddhist cultures, monastic life, temples, rituals, and communities must avoid caricature, exoticism, fantasy spectacle, or generic "Asian spiritual" aesthetics. QA must review whether visuals respect cultural specificity where relevant.

Historical visuals need not be museum-perfect in every artistic context, but they must not mislead viewers or trivialize traditions. If the asset is documentary-style, the accuracy standard rises.

Historical dignity includes avoiding anachronistic props, inappropriate clothing, inaccurate architecture, and sensational staging.

## Sacred Imagery

Sacred imagery requires reverence. Depictions of the Buddha, Bodhisattvas, stupas, sutras, altars, temples, monastics, ritual objects, and devotional scenes must be handled with restraint and respect.

Sacred imagery may be beautiful, cinematic, and emotionally resonant, but it must never be exploitative.

Lighting shapes meaning. QA must verify that lighting supports the channel's emotional palette and does not distort sacred or sensitive topics. Excessively dark, horror-like, harsh, or sensational lighting may be inappropriate for Buddhist education, especially around death, hell realms, grief, or ritual.

Gentle contrast, contemplative atmosphere, and cinematic depth are acceptable. The question is whether lighting serves reflection or manipulates fear.

Lighting should also support readability and accessibility.

Color must align with the Visual Engine and Brand Bible. QA must check for consistency, symbolic appropriateness, visual harmony, and avoidance of one-note palettes or trend-driven color systems.

Color choices around sacred figures, mourning, death, Pure Land, healing, and meditation should be reviewed carefully. Color should not imply supernatural guarantees or emotional intensity beyond the content.

Thumbnails may use stronger contrast for clarity, but not at the cost of dignity.

## Composition

Composition should guide attention without crowding, confusion, or visual disrespect. QA must review framing of sacred figures, placement of text, balance of negative space, hierarchy, readability, and mobile legibility.

Composition failures include text covering faces or sacred objects, chaotic layouts, overly dramatic close-ups, disrespectful cropping, and visual clutter that undermines calm authority.

Composition should support both aesthetic quality and educational clarity.

## Symbol Accuracy

Buddhist symbols must be used accurately and respectfully. QA must review lotuses, wheels, stupas, halos, mudras, robes, bowls, prayer beads, sutra scrolls, temple forms, Pure Land imagery, hell realm imagery, and cultural motifs.

Incorrect symbols can mislead viewers or offend communities. A mudra should not be randomly assigned if it carries meaning. Ritual objects should not be mixed without context. Sacred scripts should not be decorative gibberish.

If a symbol is used abstractly, the abstraction must still be dignified.

## AI Artifacts

AI-generated visuals require artifact review. QA must inspect hands, faces, eyes, text, scripts, sacred objects, architectural details, clothing, repeated patterns, and background elements. AI artifacts can create uncanny, disrespectful, or misleading imagery.

Any unreadable pseudo-script on sacred texts, distorted Buddha images, malformed hands in prayer, inaccurate robes, or impossible ritual objects must be rejected or corrected.

Visual polish is not enough. Sacred subjects require close inspection.

## Thumbnail Ethics

Thumbnails are high-risk growth visuals. QA must review them for promise accuracy, emotional ethics, dignity, readability, and avoidance of clickbait. A thumbnail may be compelling, but it must not manipulate fear or misrepresent the content.

Forbidden thumbnail patterns include shocked faces attached to sacred topics, horror imagery for hell realms, exaggerated karmic threat, before-after spiritual transformation, miracle bait, fake scripture, and visual claims of guaranteed healing or protection.

Thumbnail success must be measured with trust, not only CTR.

## Accessibility

Visual QA includes accessibility checks: text size, contrast, mobile readability, non-overlap, safe motion, caption compatibility, color-blind awareness, and avoidance of visual noise.

Beautiful visuals that cannot be read or processed by viewers are quality failures. Accessibility is not optional polish. It is part of compassion.

Visual accessibility must be checked across major viewing contexts, especially mobile.

# PART 8 - Production QA

## Purpose of Production QA

Production QA audits whether the studio's workflow was followed and whether the package is complete, traceable, versioned, and ready for publication or archive. It does not evaluate doctrine or style in depth unless those failures appear through production records. It ensures operational integrity.

Production QA is essential because a high-quality script or visual can still become risky if the wrong version is published, sources are missing, approvals are undocumented, or archive lineage is broken.

The reviewer must confirm that the production system can answer what the asset is, why it exists, what supports it, who approved it, where it is stored, and how it can be maintained.

## Workflow Compliance

QA must verify that required stages occurred: intake, research, planning, content creation, visual planning, review, revision, approval, publishing package assembly, distribution readiness, analytics plan, and archive preparation where applicable.

Not every asset requires every stage at the same depth, but skipped stages must be justified by risk class and documented. High-risk assets may not skip required gates.

Workflow compliance protects against invisible shortcuts.

## Artifact Completeness

The production package must include all required artifacts for its format and risk level. These may include Research Dossier, Knowledge Packet, Episode Brief, Story Outline, Audio Script, Visual Brief, Thumbnail Brief, Image Prompt Package where authorized, Voice Package, Editing Notes, Description, SEO Package where authorized, Publishing Package, Analytics Plan, Archive Package, and Version History.

This document does not create those assets. It defines that QA must verify their presence when applicable.

Missing artifacts are blockers when they affect truth, safety, publishing readiness, or future auditability.

## Metadata

QA must verify metadata such as asset ID, title field, format, series, content pillar, audience maturity, risk class, source packet, version, owner, status, approvals, publish destination, archive location, and maintenance schedule.

Metadata enables discoverability and accountability. An asset without metadata may become impossible to maintain.

Metadata must be accurate and consistent with naming conventions.

## Version Control

Version control prevents wrong-version publication. QA must confirm that the reviewed version matches the final package. If revisions occur after approval, the asset must return to QA unless the change is explicitly classified as non-material.

Version records must identify changes, date, owner, reason, affected domains, and approval status. "Final_final" style naming is not acceptable as governance.

Version failure is a serious production risk.

## Dependencies

Dependencies include source packets, series continuity records, visual references, prior episodes, glossary standards, community guidelines, publishing requirements, and accessibility files. QA must verify that dependencies are current and attached.

An asset may be blocked if it depends on an unapproved or outdated artifact. For example, a new episode in a series must not use a retired terminology standard.

Dependency review supports long-term consistency.

## Publishing Package

Before publication, QA must review the publishing package for completeness and consistency. It should include final media, title or approved naming field where applicable, description, thumbnail, tags or metadata, playlist assignment, cross-links, pinned comment where applicable, references, captions, accessibility status, release settings, and archive route.

Publishing package QA must ensure that packaging does not introduce new claims or emotional framing that were not reviewed in the main content.

Many quality failures happen at the final packaging stage. QA must treat it seriously.

## Archive Integrity

Archive integrity means that the asset can be recovered, audited, and maintained. QA must verify storage location, source records, approval records, version history, final outputs, derivatives, correction notes, and maintenance schedule.

The archive must include rejected or revised decisions where relevant. Future teams need to know what was not allowed and why.

An asset is not complete until archive integrity passes.

## Automation Logs

If automation is used for generation, checking, transformation, upload, metadata creation, analytics capture, or archive routing, QA must verify logs. Logs should identify tool, timestamp, input, output, error state, and human or AI authority where relevant.

Automation without logs creates untraceable risk. If a tool changes an asset after approval, the package must return to QA unless the change is verified as non-material.

Automation may assist quality, but it must not make invisible decisions.

Growth QA verifies that discovery, packaging, navigation, community, experiments, and analytics interpretation support learning without manipulating viewers. It protects the channel from clickbait, trend-chasing, fear marketing, and metric corruption.

Growth QA must examine trust signals such as thoughtful comments, repeat learning behavior, low mismatch complaints, willingness to share, respectful correction, and sustained engagement with sensitive topics.

Trust is the growth asset QA protects.

Clickbait is prohibited. QA must identify not only obvious clickbait but subtle forms: exaggerated curiosity, hidden subject matter, emotional bait, misleading contrast, inflated transformation, and false stakes.

The channel may be compelling. It may not be deceptive.

# PART 10 - Safety QA

Safety QA protects viewers from harm, especially when content touches vulnerable emotional states, health, mental health, death, grief, suicide, abuse, family pressure, fear, miracles, money, politics, or religious sensitivity. Safety QA is mandatory for sensitive topics.

Safety QA does not remove all difficult content. It ensures difficult content is handled responsibly and within the channel's scope.

The reviewer must ask whether a reasonable vulnerable viewer could interpret the asset in a harmful way.

Fear-based messaging uses anxiety, punishment, karmic threat, hell realms, ancestor guilt, death fear, illness fear, or spiritual insecurity to influence viewers. QA must reject it.

Buddhist teachings may discuss suffering and consequences, but must do so with compassion and context. Packaging or narration that makes viewers afraid in order to retain attention is forbidden.

Fear is a topic, not a tool.

Miracle claims require extreme caution. QA must verify whether the asset presents miracles as tradition, legend, devotional belief, symbolic narrative, historical claim, or modern claim. It must reject unsupported certainty.

The channel must not promise miracles, healing, protection, wealth, karmic cleansing, or supernatural intervention. Devotional stories may be discussed respectfully with proper framing.

Miracle sensationalism is a safety and trust risk.

False promises include guaranteed healing, peace, merit, protection, enlightenment, karmic improvement, family repair, mental health recovery, financial benefit, or spiritual success. QA must reject them.

The channel may say that teachings can support reflection, understanding, compassion, and practice orientation. It may not guarantee outcomes.

False promises exploit hope.

The channel must not provide medical advice, diagnosis, treatment recommendations, or claims that Buddhist practice can cure illness. QA must review any content involving sickness, healing, pain, medicine, or therapy.

Buddhist teachings may support meaning, compassion, and mental steadiness during illness, but must not replace professional care.

If medical topics appear, scope language may be required.

Mental health content requires careful boundaries. The channel may discuss suffering, mindfulness, compassion, grief, anxiety, and emotional life in reflective terms. It must not diagnose, treat, or instruct viewers to replace therapy, medication, crisis care, or professional support.

QA must reject language that implies meditation or devotion will cure depression, trauma, addiction, panic, or suicidal thoughts.

Mental health safety is a high-priority review area.

Financial claims are generally outside the channel's mission. QA must reject claims that Buddhist practice, merit, chanting, donations, or devotion will produce wealth, business success, debt relief, or material luck.

The channel may discuss generosity, ethics, contentment, right livelihood, and responsibility, but not transactional promises.

Financial superstition is forbidden.

The channel should avoid partisan politics unless a topic requires historically or ethically relevant context. QA must review political references for neutrality, necessity, and risk.

Buddhist ethics may speak to compassion, non-harm, responsibility, and social conduct, but the channel should not become a political persuasion platform.

Interfaith references must be respectful. QA must reject dismissive comparisons, superiority claims, caricatures, or attempts to convert through criticism of other religions.

The channel may explain Buddhist differences clearly, but should do so without contempt.

Religious sensitivity protects both viewers and the Dharma's public representation.

Cultural respect applies to Vietnamese Buddhism, broader Mahayana contexts, Theravada communities, Vajrayana traditions, East Asian traditions, South Asian historical settings, and modern global audiences. QA must reject cultural flattening, exoticism, and misuse of sacred forms.

The channel should not turn cultures into scenery. Cultural elements must have purpose and respect.

Suicide-related content is critical risk. QA must escalate any direct suicide discussion to human review and crisis-handling standards. The channel must not provide methods, romanticize death, imply spiritual benefit from suicide, shame suicidal people, or present simplistic spiritual solutions.

Crisis language must be clear, compassionate, and bounded. QA must reject vague spiritual reassurance as a substitute for crisis support. It must also reject panic, blame, or shame.

# PART 13 - Scoring System

## Purpose of Scoring

The QA scoring system creates a repeatable structure for assessing quality across domains. It helps reviewers evaluate artifacts consistently, identify weak areas, compare readiness across versions, detect recurring failure patterns, and support transparent decisions.

Scoring is not a substitute for judgment. A numerical score cannot capture all doctrinal nuance, emotional sensitivity, or trust risk. Scores must always be accompanied by evidence and written reasoning. The score summarizes review; it does not replace review.

The scoring system must be weighted by risk. A low-risk internal formatting issue should not block an otherwise ready asset. A low Buddhist accuracy score must block publication even if all other scores are strong. Domain-specific blockers override aggregate averages.

## Score Scale

All domains use a 0 to 5 score unless a domain is marked not applicable. The canonical interpretation is:

0 means absent, fabricated, unsafe, or unusable.

1 means severe failure requiring rejection or complete reconstruction.

2 means significant weakness requiring major revision.

3 means acceptable with revisions or caution, depending on risk.

4 means strong and publishable with minor or no revision.

5 means excellent, well supported, exemplary, and reusable as a standard reference.

Reviewers must avoid inflated scores. A score of 5 should be rare and evidence-rich.

## Research Quality

Research Quality evaluates source hierarchy, citation integrity, claim support, translation quality, confidence scoring, cross-references, and hallucination resistance.

A score below 3 blocks any asset that contains factual, doctrinal, historical, scientific, medical, or safety-relevant claims. A score of 3 may permit conditional progress only if weaknesses are documented and not public-facing. A score of 4 or 5 supports publication, subject to other gates.

Research Quality should be weighted heavily for scripture, history, doctrine, psychology, and sensitive topics.

## Content Quality

Content Quality evaluates storytelling, structure, clarity, tone, educational value, audience fit, emotional pacing, and calls to action. It asks whether the viewer can receive the teaching responsibly.

A score below 3 requires revision. A content score of 3 may be acceptable for simple low-risk assets if accuracy and safety are strong, but major public assets should target 4 or higher.

Content Quality cannot compensate for Buddhist or research failure.

## Buddhist Accuracy

Buddhist Accuracy evaluates canonical correctness, doctrinal neutrality, tradition awareness, terminology, interpretation, context preservation, and absence of invented teachings.

This is a blocking domain. Any score below 4 for public doctrinal content requires revision or escalation. A score below 3 blocks publication. A score of 5 requires strong source alignment and exemplary humility.

Buddhist Accuracy carries veto weight because it protects the Dharma and viewer trust.

## Historical Accuracy

Historical Accuracy evaluates evidence, dating, context, source distinction, legendary versus historical framing, and avoidance of unsupported certainty.

Public historical claims should score at least 4. A score of 3 may be acceptable only when the asset clearly frames uncertainty and the historical claim is not central. Scores below 3 block publication.

Historical Accuracy is especially important for documentary-style content and the life of the Buddha.

## Visual Quality

Visual Quality evaluates dignity, composition, character consistency, symbol accuracy, lighting, color, accessibility, AI artifacts, and thumbnail ethics.

A score below 3 blocks visual publication. A score of 3 requires revision unless the visual is minor and low-risk. Sacred imagery, thumbnails, and high-visibility visuals should target 4 or higher.

Visual Quality includes ethical dignity, not only aesthetics.

## Production Quality

Production Quality evaluates workflow compliance, artifact completeness, metadata, version control, dependencies, publishing package, archive integrity, and automation logs.

A score below 3 blocks publication because the asset cannot be reliably managed. A score of 3 may allow conditional approval only if missing items are low-risk and scheduled for completion before release.

Production Quality ensures traceability.

## Growth Quality

Growth Quality evaluates titles, descriptions, playlists, recommendations, community strategy, experiment ethics, CTR ethics, retention ethics, and trust signals.

A score below 3 blocks public packaging. A score of 3 requires revision if packaging risks mismatch or weak trust. High-stakes discovery surfaces should target 4 or higher.

Growth Quality protects the viewer's first impression.

## Safety

Safety evaluates fear-based messaging, false promises, medical and mental health boundaries, crisis risk, religious sensitivity, children, bereavement, suicide-related content, cultural respect, and emotional safety.

Safety is a blocking domain. Any score below 4 on sensitive topics requires revision or escalation. A score below 3 creates Emergency Hold for public release if harm risk is plausible.

Safety must never be averaged away.

## Accessibility

Accessibility evaluates language clarity, reading level, subtitles, audio clarity, visual readability, color contrast, mobile experience, international usability, and inclusive language.

A score below 3 requires revision. A score of 3 may pass for low-risk internal or transitional assets, but public educational assets should target 4. Accessibility issues that prevent comprehension are blockers.

Accessibility is part of compassion and educational value.

## Brand Consistency

Brand Consistency evaluates voice, mission, tone, identity, visual DNA, educational positioning, and community expectations.

A score below 3 requires revision. A score of 3 may pass only if deviation is minor and documented. Repeated brand scores of 3 should trigger continuity review.

Brand Consistency protects long-term recognition and trust.

## Overall Confidence

Overall Confidence summarizes whether the reviewer believes the asset is ready for its intended use. It is not an average. It must consider domain scores, blockers, risk class, evidence quality, remaining uncertainty, and reviewer authority.

Overall Confidence may be low even when many domain scores are high if one critical uncertainty remains. It may also be high for a simple low-risk asset with fewer applicable domains.

Overall Confidence must include a written rationale.

## Score Thresholds

For Low Risk assets, no applicable domain may score below 3, and overall confidence must be at least 3.5.

For Medium Risk assets, all applicable domains should score at least 3.5, and Buddhist, safety, research, and brand domains must score at least 4 where applicable.

For High Risk assets, all critical domains must score at least 4, overall confidence must be at least 4, and human or specialized review may be required.

For Critical Risk assets, numerical scoring is not enough. The asset requires escalation, explicit human decision, and documented risk acceptance or rejection.

# PART 14 - Decision System

## Purpose of Decision System

The decision system converts QA findings into action. It defines what happens after review. Decisions must be clear, enforceable, documented, and matched to risk.

The QA Engine must avoid ambiguous outcomes. "Looks fine" is not a decision. "Needs work" is not enough. Every decision must specify status, conditions, required actions, re-review needs, and authority.

The decision system protects production from uncertainty.

## Approve

Approve means the asset meets all required standards for its intended use. No blocking issues remain. Minor non-blocking observations may be recorded for future improvement, but they do not affect release.

Approval must identify scope, version, date, reviewer, and applicable formats. Approval for one format does not automatically approve derivatives.

Approve should be used only when the reviewer would be comfortable defending the asset publicly and internally.

## Approve with Minor Revisions

Approve with Minor Revisions means the asset is fundamentally ready, but small corrections must be made before release. These corrections must not affect meaning, doctrine, safety, or public promise.

Examples include minor formatting, typo correction, link cleanup, caption timing adjustment, metadata normalization, or small readability improvement.

The decision must state whether QA must verify the revisions or whether production may confirm completion. If the revision touches meaning, this outcome is not appropriate.

## Conditional Approval

Conditional Approval means the asset may proceed only if specified conditions are met before publication. Conditions may include adding a source note, adjusting a description, replacing a visual, improving captions, attaching archive metadata, or receiving a domain-specific signoff.

Conditional Approval must include deadlines, owners, and re-review requirements. It must not become a way to publish unresolved risk.

If conditions are not met, approval is void.

## Major Revision Required

Major Revision Required means the asset has significant issues but remains potentially viable. It must return to the owning engine for substantial changes and then undergo re-review.

Major revision may be required for weak structure, unclear teaching, unsupported claims, inappropriate tone, visual dignity problems, unsafe emotional framing, incomplete research, or packaging mismatch.

The decision must distinguish required changes from optional suggestions. Owners must know what will block approval.

## Reject

Reject means the asset should not proceed in its current concept, framing, or execution. Rejection may occur because the topic is unsuitable, the approach violates mission, the source base is insufficient, the risk is too high, the visual treatment is unacceptable, or the asset cannot be corrected without rebuilding.

Reject does not necessarily mean the general subject can never be addressed. It means this artifact or approach is not acceptable.

Rejected decisions must be archived so future teams do not repeat the same failure.

## Emergency Hold

Emergency Hold stops publication or distribution immediately. It is used when an asset presents critical risk: fabricated scripture, dangerous medical or mental health implication, suicide risk mishandling, severe doctrinal distortion, defamatory or inflammatory content, disrespectful sacred imagery, major publishing error, or trust-threatening misinformation.

Emergency Hold may apply before publication or after release. If already published, the studio must consider takedown, correction, description update, pinned clarification, or replacement according to severity.

Emergency Hold requires escalation and documentation.

## Escalate to Human Review

Escalate to Human Review means QA cannot responsibly decide through standard review alone. Escalation is required for high-risk doctrinal disputes, suicide-related content, abuse and trauma, serious medical implications, contested historical claims, legal or platform risk, major public correction, or conflict among governing standards.

The escalation package must include evidence, issue summary, options, risk level, recommendation, and deadline constraints.

Until human review resolves the issue, the asset remains blocked unless a qualified authority explicitly authorizes limited action.

## Decision Logic

Decision logic follows the strictest applicable domain. If any blocking domain fails, the asset cannot be approved. If safety fails, publication stops. If Buddhist accuracy fails, doctrinal content stops. If research fails, claims stop. If production traceability fails, publishing stops. If growth packaging fails, public discovery surfaces stop.

High average scores cannot override critical failures. A beautiful, engaging, accessible video with one fabricated quote is not publishable. A well-researched script with manipulative thumbnail packaging is not publishable as a package.

QA decisions must be conservative where viewer harm or Dharma distortion is plausible.

# PART 15 - Audit Trail

## Purpose of Audit Trail

The audit trail is the permanent record of QA reasoning and decisions. It allows future teams to understand what was reviewed, what evidence was considered, what risks were identified, what revisions occurred, and why an asset was approved or blocked.

Audit trails protect accountability. They also support learning. Without audit trails, the studio cannot identify recurring failures, defend decisions, correct errors, or maintain consistency across years.

Every public asset must have an audit trail.

## Reviewer

Audit records must identify the reviewer or reviewing system. If an AI agent performed preliminary review, that must be labeled. If a human reviewer approved high-risk content, the record must identify their role and authority.

Anonymous approval is not acceptable. Accountability requires known review responsibility.

Reviewer records should include domain of review, not only name or agent ID.

## Timestamp

Audit records must include timestamps for submission, review, decision, revision, re-review, approval, and archive completion. Time records support version control and incident investigation.

Timestamps should use a consistent format. They should be attached to specific asset versions.

If an asset changes after approval, timestamp comparison should reveal the issue.

## Evidence

Audit records must include evidence reviewed. Evidence may include citations, source excerpts within copyright limits, source identifiers, screenshots, visual references, checklists, score sheets, analytics reports, accessibility test results, and prior decisions.

Evidence must be sufficient for future reviewers to understand the basis of the decision. A note saying "verified" is not enough.

Evidence should be linked, not merely described, where possible.

## Reasoning

Audit reasoning explains why the decision was made. It should identify standards applied, risks considered, alternatives rejected, and remaining uncertainty.

Reasoning must be concise but substantive. Future teams need to understand the logic, not read a vague conclusion.

Reasoning is especially important for conditional approvals, rejected assets, high-risk content, and exceptions.

## Confidence

Audit records must include confidence level. Confidence should reflect source strength, reviewer certainty, risk class, unresolved issues, and scope of approval.

Low or medium confidence may be acceptable only when the asset is scoped accordingly and risks are non-blocking. High-risk public claims require high confidence or explicit uncertainty language.

Confidence records prevent future agents from treating cautious decisions as absolute certainty.

## Linked Assets

Audit records must link to all relevant assets: source packet, script, visual package, publishing package, thumbnail, description, captions, playlist, community post, analytics report, and archive package where applicable.

Linked assets allow reviewers to reconstruct the full public package. A script approval without thumbnail or title review is incomplete for publication.

Asset links must remain stable.

## Revision History

Revision history records what changed, why, who changed it, which QA finding it addressed, and whether re-review occurred. It must distinguish minor edits from material revisions.

Material revisions include changes to claims, tone, structure, visual meaning, title promise, safety language, source citation, or publishing context.

Revision history is essential for post-publication correction.

## Audit Log Schema

Every QA audit log should include asset ID, asset title field or working name, format, series, owner, version, risk class, submission date, reviewer, review domains, domain scores, findings, evidence links, decision, required actions, revision records, re-review notes, final approval status, archive location, and maintenance triggers.

The schema must be stable enough for long-term analysis. Additional fields may be added, but core fields should not be removed without governance review.

# PART 16 - Continuous Improvement

## Purpose of Continuous Improvement

Continuous improvement turns QA findings into stronger systems. QA should not only catch errors. It should reduce future errors by identifying patterns, strengthening templates, updating guidance, training agents, improving workflows, and refining governance.

A mature QA system becomes more useful over time. It learns where failures occur and designs prevention.

Continuous improvement must remain evidence-based. Anecdotes may trigger investigation, but recurring patterns should guide systemic changes.

## Recurring Audits

The studio must conduct recurring audits of published content, active series, high-risk topics, visual standards, accessibility, growth packaging, and archive integrity. Audits help detect drift that individual reviews may miss.

Audit cadence should depend on risk and importance. Foundational and high-traffic assets require more frequent review than low-risk peripheral assets.

Recurring audits must produce actions, not only reports.

## Trend Analysis

Trend analysis examines repeated QA findings over time. It may reveal recurring citation weakness, title exaggeration, terminology inconsistency, visual artifact patterns, accessibility gaps, or community safety issues.

Trend analysis should identify whether failures originate in research, briefing, writing, visual generation, production handoff, growth optimization, or final publishing.

The studio should fix systems before blaming individuals or agents.

## Root Cause Analysis

Root cause analysis is required for serious or repeated failures. It asks why the failure occurred, why it was not caught earlier, what controls failed, and how recurrence will be prevented.

Root causes may include unclear standards, incomplete templates, weak training, missing metadata, insufficient review authority, schedule pressure, tool limitations, or ambiguous ownership.

A correction without root cause analysis may allow the same failure to return.

## Common Failures

Common failures should be cataloged. Likely categories include unsupported claims, overconfident doctrine, fabricated quotes, weak translation, tradition mixing, emotional manipulation, vague wellness language, visual artifacts, thumbnail mismatch, missing metadata, inaccessible captions, and archive gaps.

The catalog should include examples, risk severity, detection method, and prevention rule.

Common failure records help onboard future agents.

## Knowledge Updates

QA findings may require updates to the knowledge system. If a terminology problem recurs, the glossary may need revision. If a doctrinal distinction is repeatedly misunderstood, the Buddhist Guide or Research Engine may need clarification. If a visual symbol is misused, the Visual Engine may need stronger rules.

QA cannot unilaterally rewrite governing documents unless authorized, but it can recommend updates through the governance process.

Knowledge updates must be versioned.

## Quality Feedback Loops

QA feedback must flow back to the relevant engine. Research failures go to Research Engine. Content failures go to Content Engine. Visual failures go to Visual Engine. Workflow failures go to Production Engine. Packaging failures go to Growth Engine. Brand failures go to Brand governance. Safety failures may affect multiple systems.

Feedback must be specific enough to change behavior. A note saying "improve quality" is useless.

The loop closes only when the engine changes practice or explains why no change is needed.

## Training Improvements

Future AI agents and human contributors must be trained on QA findings. Training updates may include improved checklists, examples of rejected language, source verification exercises, visual inspection standards, accessibility rules, and escalation scenarios.

Training should include both positive and negative examples. Agents need to see what good looks like and what failure looks like.

Training improvements must be documented in onboarding materials.

# PART 17 - Risk Management

## Purpose of Risk Management

Risk management classifies potential harm and determines review intensity. Not all assets require the same process. A low-risk community navigation note should not receive the same review burden as a video about suicide, karma, or hell realms. However, the system must know the difference before work proceeds.

Risk classification must consider topic, audience vulnerability, claim type, format, distribution scale, source certainty, visual intensity, and potential for misunderstanding.

Risk should be assigned early and updated when the asset changes.

## Low Risk

Low Risk assets have minimal potential for doctrinal, factual, emotional, safety, or reputational harm. Examples may include routine navigation text, low-stakes evergreen reminders, simple archive metadata, or minor formatting updates.

Low Risk handling may use standard QA review, simplified scoring, and production verification. However, low risk does not mean no review. Even simple assets can introduce brand inconsistency or broken links.

If a low-risk asset adds a claim, it may become medium risk.

## Medium Risk

Medium Risk assets include public educational content with manageable claims, standard Buddhist explanations, routine visual packages, playlists, descriptions, and community prompts that do not involve highly sensitive topics.

Medium Risk handling requires domain QA for applicable areas, evidence review, score thresholds, and archive logging. Human review may not be required unless the reviewer identifies uncertainty.

Most normal production will fall into Medium Risk.

## High Risk

High Risk assets involve doctrinal complexity, sensitive emotional states, death, grief, karma and suffering, rebirth, hell realms, miracles, ritual claims, mental health boundaries, family conflict, abuse, children, contested history, major sacred imagery, or high-visibility growth packaging.

High Risk handling requires specialized domain QA, stronger evidence, explicit safety review, careful language, likely human signoff, and full audit trail. High-risk assets should not be rushed.

High risk does not mean forbidden. It means handled with care.

## Critical Risk

Critical Risk assets involve plausible immediate harm, crisis content, suicide-related material, dangerous medical or mental health implications, fabricated scripture in a public asset, severe doctrinal distortion, serious cultural or religious offense, legal risk, or major public misinformation.

Critical Risk handling requires Emergency Hold, escalation to qualified human review, executive awareness where appropriate, and documented resolution. Publication is blocked until the issue is resolved.

If already published, critical risk may require takedown, correction, or public clarification.

## Handling Procedures

Every risk level must define required reviewers, required evidence, scoring thresholds, escalation triggers, approval authority, archive requirements, and maintenance schedule.

Risk handling must be visible in the production package. Downstream agents must know the risk classification and cannot downgrade it without approval.

Risk classification may increase at any stage. It should rarely decrease without strong evidence.

## Risk Register

The QA Engine must maintain a Risk Register. It records high-risk and critical-risk issues, affected assets, severity, owner, decision, mitigation, resolution, and recurrence prevention.

The Risk Register supports trend analysis and governance review. It helps the studio see whether certain topics, agents, formats, or workflows repeatedly generate risk.

Risk records must be treated as learning tools, not blame artifacts.

# PART 18 - Future-proof QA

## Future-Proofing Principle

Future-proof QA means the studio can adopt new AI models, research, tools, platforms, formats, and workflows without lowering standards. QA principles remain stable even when execution changes.

The core principles are evidence before confidence, humility before certainty, compassion before performance, trust before publishing, and traceability before scale. These principles must survive every technological transition.

Future tools may accelerate review, but they must not remove accountability.

## New AI Models

New AI models may improve reasoning, writing, visual generation, translation, moderation, and analytics. They may also introduce new failure modes. QA must evaluate models before relying on them for production-critical work.

Model evaluation should include hallucination tendency, citation reliability, doctrinal sensitivity, tone consistency, visual artifact rate, bias patterns, safety handling, and ability to follow governing documents.

No model may self-certify its own outputs for high-risk content.

## New Buddhist Research

New scholarship, translations, and community knowledge may affect how topics should be explained. QA must allow the studio to update standards when credible new information appears.

New research should be reviewed through the Research Engine and Buddhist QA before changing public content. The studio must avoid sudden shifts based on weak sources.

When older content becomes outdated, QA must classify the maintenance need and decide whether refresh, update, correction, or replacement is required.

## New Production Tools

New production tools may change editing, asset generation, captioning, audio processing, archive management, or publishing. QA must verify that tools preserve version control, metadata, accessibility, and audit logs.

A tool that improves speed but hides changes is risky. A tool that creates beautiful visuals but frequent sacred inaccuracies requires strict review.

Tool adoption must include quality testing.

## New Publishing Platforms

New platforms may require different formats, metadata, moderation approaches, or viewer interactions. QA must translate standards into platform-specific checks without weakening them.

If a platform rewards sensationalism or unsafe compression, QA may restrict how the studio uses it. Platform fit is a quality decision.

No platform may bypass QA approval.

## Future Media Formats

Future formats may include interactive experiences, immersive media, adaptive learning, conversational agents, live events, courses, books, or formats not yet common. QA must evaluate them by truth, dignity, safety, accessibility, traceability, and viewer agency.

Immersive representations of sacred figures, ritual, Pure Land, hell realms, or death require heightened review. Interactivity can create emotional power and therefore emotional risk.

Future media must deepen learning, not turn Buddhism into spectacle.

## Without Lowering Standards

The studio may simplify workflows, automate checks, improve tooling, and increase throughput, but standards may not be lowered for convenience. If a new workflow cannot meet existing standards, the workflow must be revised or rejected.

QA must watch for gradual weakening disguised as modernization. Faster does not mean better. More scalable does not mean more trustworthy.

The studio's reputation depends on standards that remain firm.

## Permanent QA Charter

The QA Engine's permanent charter is:

**Protect truth. Protect the Dharma. Protect viewers. Protect trust. Protect the library. Protect the future studio from the mistakes of the present.**

Every reviewer, human or AI, must act from this charter. QA must be fair, specific, evidence-based, transparent, and compassionate. It must not become arbitrary or ego-driven. It must also not become timid. When something is unsafe, unsupported, misleading, disrespectful, inaccessible, or inconsistent, QA must say so clearly.

Nothing may bypass this system.

No deadline outranks truth.

No metric outranks compassion.

No visual impact outranks dignity.

No AI fluency outranks evidence.

No human preference outranks governing standards.

No publication outranks viewer trust.

# PART 19 - Reviewer Role System

## Purpose of Reviewer Roles

The QA Engine requires clear reviewer roles because quality is multidimensional. A single reviewer may be skilled in narrative but weak in Buddhist doctrine. Another may understand source hierarchy but miss accessibility issues. Another may detect visual artifacts but not growth manipulation. The studio must avoid the false efficiency of one-person universal approval for complex assets.

Reviewer roles define authority, responsibility, limits, escalation duties, and evidence expectations. They also prevent review collapse, where one approval is treated as approval across all domains. A long-term Buddhist media studio needs specialized review lanes that can work together without confusion.

Reviewer roles may be filled by qualified humans, AI-assisted systems, or hybrid processes depending on risk. However, high-risk and critical-risk decisions require appropriate human oversight where governing standards demand it. AI can support review, but it cannot become the sole final authority over sensitive doctrinal, safety, or crisis material.

## Lead QA Reviewer

The Lead QA Reviewer owns the final coordination of QA for an asset. This role confirms that all required domain reviews have occurred, blockers are resolved, scores are recorded, decisions are coherent, and archive records are complete.

The Lead QA Reviewer does not need to personally perform every domain review. Their responsibility is integration. They must ensure that no domain finding is ignored and that the final decision reflects the strictest applicable standard.

The Lead QA Reviewer may not override a specialist blocker without documented escalation. If Buddhist QA fails, the Lead QA Reviewer cannot approve based on strong production quality. If safety QA fails, the Lead QA Reviewer must hold the asset.

## Buddhist Reviewer

The Buddhist Reviewer evaluates doctrine, scripture use, terminology, tradition awareness, devotional framing, practice representation, karma language, rebirth language, merit language, and sacred figures. This role requires familiarity with the Buddhist Guide and relevant source hierarchy.

The Buddhist Reviewer must distinguish error from legitimate interpretive range. They should not impose personal sectarian preference where multiple responsible interpretations exist. They must also reject false neutrality when an asset clearly misrepresents a tradition.

This role has blocking authority over Buddhist claims. If doctrinal risk remains unresolved, the asset cannot publish.

## Research Reviewer

The Research Reviewer evaluates whether claims are sourced, cited, cross-referenced, and appropriately scoped. This role checks evidence quality, hallucination risk, historical support, translation notes, and confidence levels.

The Research Reviewer must be skeptical of fluency. They should trace claims to sources and identify unsupported expansions introduced during writing or packaging.

This role has blocking authority over factual and source-dependent claims. If evidence is insufficient, the claim must be revised, qualified, removed, or escalated.

## Editorial Reviewer

The Editorial Reviewer evaluates clarity, narrative flow, tone, emotional progression, structure, audience fit, and educational value. This role ensures the asset teaches well and feels aligned with the channel's reflective identity.

The Editorial Reviewer must not prioritize dramatic effectiveness over truth. They should identify where narrative choices distort meaning or create unsafe emotional pressure.

This role has authority to require content revision when the asset is confusing, misaligned, manipulative, underdeveloped, or poorly structured.

## Visual Reviewer

The Visual Reviewer evaluates imagery, composition, color, typography, sacred representation, historical dignity, AI artifacts, thumbnail ethics, and accessibility. This role applies the Visual Engine and Brand Bible.

The Visual Reviewer must inspect details carefully, especially in AI-generated imagery. Sacred text, hands, faces, robes, mudras, ritual objects, and architecture require close review.

This role has blocking authority over visual dignity and public visual readiness.

## Safety Reviewer

The Safety Reviewer evaluates harm potential. This includes crisis language, suicide-related content, grief, bereavement, abuse, trauma, medical and mental health boundaries, fear-based messaging, false promises, children, and vulnerable viewers.

The Safety Reviewer must use the most protective reasonable interpretation. If language could plausibly be misunderstood in a harmful way by a vulnerable viewer, it must be revised or escalated.

This role has veto authority for safety-critical issues.

## Accessibility Reviewer

The Accessibility Reviewer evaluates whether viewers can perceive, read, hear, understand, and navigate the asset. This includes subtitles, captions, audio clarity, contrast, mobile readability, language clarity, international accessibility, and inclusive language.

The Accessibility Reviewer must consider real viewing conditions, including mobile screens, noisy environments, hearing limitations, visual limitations, and unfamiliarity with Buddhist terminology.

This role can block release when accessibility failures prevent comprehension or create exclusion.

## Growth Ethics Reviewer

The Growth Ethics Reviewer evaluates public packaging and distribution surfaces. This includes titles, descriptions, thumbnails as discovery assets, playlists, recommendations, community prompts, experiments, and analytics interpretation.

The Growth Ethics Reviewer must identify clickbait, fear marketing, false urgency, exaggerated benefit, mismatch between promise and content, and metric corruption.

This role can block packaging even when the core content is approved. A good video with manipulative packaging is not publishable as a final package.

## Archive Reviewer

The Archive Reviewer verifies that the asset's quality record can survive time. This role checks version history, audit logs, source packet linkage, final outputs, derivatives, correction records, metadata, and maintenance triggers.

The Archive Reviewer ensures that future teams can understand and reuse the asset responsibly. If archive records are incomplete, the asset may be operationally unsafe even if content is strong.

This role protects long-term maintainability.

## Reviewer Independence

Reviewer independence is essential. The creator of an asset should not be the only final reviewer of that asset. AI agents should not approve their own outputs for high-risk or public release. Human editors should disclose when they have created, revised, or strongly advocated for the work being reviewed.

Independence does not require hostility. It requires enough separation to see errors clearly.

When independence is impossible due to limited staffing, the audit record must state the limitation and apply stricter checklist discipline.

# PART 20 - QA Issue Taxonomy

## Purpose of Issue Taxonomy

The QA Issue Taxonomy standardizes how problems are named, classified, and tracked. Without a shared taxonomy, reviewers may describe the same issue in different ways, making trend analysis difficult. A taxonomy allows the studio to see patterns across hundreds of assets.

Each issue should be assigned a category, severity, affected domain, evidence, required action, owner, and resolution status. The taxonomy supports both individual decisions and long-term quality improvement.

Issue taxonomy must remain practical. It should help reviewers think clearly, not bury them in labels.

## Doctrinal Issue

A doctrinal issue occurs when Buddhist teaching is misstated, oversimplified, invented, decontextualized, falsely universalized, or presented with sectarian bias. Doctrinal issues include wrong karma language, misleading rebirth claims, transactional merit framing, incorrect emptiness interpretation, false Pure Land claims, and misuse of Bodhisattva concepts.

Doctrinal issues are generally medium to critical severity depending on visibility and harm potential. Public doctrinal errors are never low priority.

Required actions may include correction, source review, Buddhist reviewer escalation, terminology revision, or asset rejection.

## Source Issue

A source issue occurs when evidence is missing, weak, misused, fabricated, incorrectly cited, or insufficient for the claim. Source issues include broken citations, unsupported quotes, vague references, wrong scripture attribution, and overreliance on low-quality secondary material.

Source issues become more severe when attached to scripture, history, science, medicine, mental health, or safety claims.

Required actions include stronger sourcing, claim reduction, citation correction, confidence downgrade, or removal.

## Historical Issue

A historical issue occurs when an asset presents uncertain, legendary, devotional, or symbolic material as historical fact, or when dates, locations, figures, events, or cultural contexts are incorrect.

Historical issues can damage credibility and disrespect tradition by confusing categories. They are especially important in documentary-style content.

Required actions include reclassification, uncertainty language, historical review, or removal of unsupported specificity.

## Translation Issue

A translation issue occurs when a term, passage, title, name, or concept is translated inaccurately, inconsistently, or too narrowly for context. It may also involve missing Vietnamese diacritics, inconsistent terminology, or translating Buddhist terms into generic wellness language.

Translation issues can become doctrinal issues when meaning changes.

Required actions include terminology correction, glossary alignment, translation note, or specialist review.

## Narrative Issue

A narrative issue occurs when story structure, pacing, emotional development, or dramatic framing weakens understanding or distorts meaning. Narrative issues include melodrama, false villains, unresolved educational promises, excessive repetition, manipulative suspense, or unclear transitions.

Narrative issues are often correctable through editorial revision.

Severity increases when narrative distortion affects doctrine, history, or emotional safety.

## Tone Issue

A tone issue occurs when the asset sounds inconsistent with the brand or inappropriate for the subject. Examples include aggressive urgency, casual sacred references, guru-like certainty, punitive language, sentimental excess, sarcasm, or platform-style loudness.

Tone issues can become trust issues. They may also become safety issues when directed at vulnerable viewers.

Required actions include voice revision, brand review, or content reframing.

## Safety Issue

A safety issue occurs when language, imagery, structure, or recommendations could harm viewers. Safety issues include suicide mishandling, medical advice, mental health overreach, fear-based karma framing, false promises, spiritual pressure, trauma mishandling, and unsafe family guidance.

Safety issues are high priority. Some require Emergency Hold.

Required actions include safety rewrite, removal, crisis escalation, human review, or publication block.

## Visual Issue

A visual issue occurs when imagery fails dignity, accuracy, accessibility, consistency, or ethical packaging standards. Examples include distorted sacred figures, inaccurate robes, pseudo-script on sutras, horror-style hell imagery, disrespectful cropping, unreadable text, or clickbait thumbnails.

Visual issues may be aesthetic, doctrinal, cultural, or safety-related. The issue category should name the actual risk, not only "visual problem."

Required actions include regeneration, redesign, reference correction, visual review, or rejection.

## Accessibility Issue

An accessibility issue prevents viewers from perceiving, understanding, or navigating the asset. Examples include poor captions, low contrast, small text, noisy audio, fast speech, unexplained terminology, complex language for beginners, and mobile readability failures.

Accessibility issues should be treated as quality failures, not optional improvements.

Required actions include caption correction, audio remix, design adjustment, language simplification, glossary support, or format-specific adaptation.

## Growth Issue

A growth issue occurs when packaging, recommendation, experiment, or analytics interpretation compromises trust. Examples include clickbait, title mismatch, fear-based discovery, misleading thumbnail, inappropriate recommendation chain, or vanity metric optimization.

Growth issues can damage the viewer relationship before content begins.

Required actions include packaging revision, experiment cancellation, recommendation redesign, or growth ethics escalation.

## Production Issue

A production issue occurs when workflow, metadata, versioning, dependencies, publishing package, automation logs, or archive records are incomplete or inconsistent.

Production issues create traceability risk. They may seem administrative, but they can cause wrong-version release, missing corrections, broken links, or unmaintainable assets.

Required actions include package repair, metadata update, version reconciliation, archive completion, or workflow root cause analysis.

# PART 21 - Corrective Action System

## Purpose of Corrective Actions

Corrective actions convert QA findings into specific work. A finding without a corrective action leaves the system unclear. The Corrective Action System defines how issues are repaired, who owns repair, when re-review is required, and how resolution is documented.

Corrective action must be proportional. Minor typos should not trigger full reconstruction. Doctrinal errors should not be handled as copy edits. Safety risks should not be treated as optional polish.

Every corrective action must connect to an identified issue.

## Correction Types

The studio uses several correction types: edit, clarify, qualify, remove, replace, restructure, re-source, re-translate, re-design, re-mix, re-caption, repackage, escalate, hold, retire, or publicly correct.

Edit is used for minor wording or formatting. Clarify adds needed explanation. Qualify adds uncertainty or scope. Remove deletes unsupported or unsafe material. Replace substitutes a better source, image, line, or asset. Restructure changes flow. Re-source rebuilds evidence. Re-translate fixes terminology. Re-design fixes visuals. Re-mix fixes audio. Re-caption fixes subtitles. Repackage fixes titles, descriptions, thumbnails, or pathways. Escalate sends the issue to higher authority. Hold pauses release. Retire removes an asset from active use. Publicly correct addresses already published errors.

The correction type must match the problem.

## Owner Assignment

Every corrective action must have an owner. Owners may include Research Engine, Content Engine, Visual Engine, Production Engine, Growth Engine, Accessibility Reviewer, Safety Reviewer, Lead QA Reviewer, or human editorial authority.

QA identifies required action but normally does not perform the correction. This separation preserves independence. If QA performs a minor correction, the audit trail must record it and determine whether independent verification is needed.

Unowned corrective actions are not actionable.

## Due Date and Priority

Corrective actions must have priority and due date. Priority depends on severity, publication status, audience exposure, risk class, and dependency. A critical published error requires immediate action. A low-risk internal metadata gap may be scheduled.

Due dates must be realistic and must not pressure reviewers to approve incomplete work. If a deadline cannot be met safely, the asset should remain held.

Priority records support production planning.

## Verification

Verification confirms that the corrective action was completed and did not introduce new issues. Verification may be performed by the original reviewer, a specialist reviewer, or the Lead QA Reviewer depending on severity.

Verification must compare the revised artifact against the original finding. It should not rely on owner assurance alone.

For material changes, verification becomes re-review.

## Recurrence Prevention

For high-severity or repeated issues, corrective action must include recurrence prevention. This may involve template changes, checklist additions, agent training, source rules, visual reference updates, or workflow gating.

The goal is not only to fix the current asset. It is to prevent the same failure from appearing again.

Recurrence prevention must be logged in continuous improvement records.

## Public Corrections

When an already published asset contains a meaningful error, QA must determine whether public correction is required. Options include description correction, pinned comment clarification, edited reupload, replacement asset, takedown, community note, article update, or future correction content where appropriate.

The correction should be honest and proportionate. The studio should not hide meaningful mistakes. It also should not over-amplify minor issues in ways that confuse viewers.

Public correction preserves trust when handled with humility.

# PART 22 - Post-Publication QA

## Purpose of Post-Publication QA

QA does not end at publication. Public release creates new information: viewer response, comment patterns, correction reports, retention signals, accessibility issues, platform rendering problems, and cross-platform behavior. Post-Publication QA monitors these signals and determines whether action is needed.

Post-publication review is especially important for high-risk topics, major series launches, new formats, sensitive visuals, and growth experiments.

The studio must remain responsible for assets after release.

## Immediate Release Check

After publication, the studio must verify that the correct version is live, the title and description match approval, the thumbnail is correct, captions work, audio is intact, links function, playlist assignment is correct, comments are configured as intended, and archive records are updated with live URLs.

Immediate release failures can undermine quality even when pre-publication review was strong.

If a wrong version or unapproved package element is live, QA may issue Emergency Hold or correction.

## Early Signal Review

Early signal review occurs after initial audience exposure. It examines mismatch complaints, confusion, harmful interpretations, accessibility reports, comment tone, moderation needs, and unexpected emotional reactions.

Early signals should not cause panic. Some disagreement or confusion may be normal. QA must distinguish normal response from quality risk.

If viewers consistently misunderstand a key teaching, the asset may need clarification or pathway support.

## Comment-Based QA Signals

Comments can reveal errors, confusion, distress, misinformation, or trust problems. QA should review comment samples for high-risk assets and classify issues through the Community Intelligence system.

A single comment may trigger investigation if it identifies a plausible factual or safety issue. Repeated comments indicate stronger signal.

Comment review must respect viewer privacy and avoid overreacting to isolated hostility.

## Analytics-Based QA Signals

Analytics may reveal quality problems: severe early drop-off, title mismatch, unusual dislike ratio, poor subtitle engagement, low continuation from expected pathways, or high clicks with low satisfaction.

Analytics do not prove failure by themselves. They raise questions. QA must interpret them with content function, audience maturity, and qualitative signals.

Analytics-based QA should avoid metric panic and focus on learning.

## Published Error Handling

When a published error is discovered, QA must classify severity, determine exposure, identify affected assets, decide correction path, assign owner, and document resolution.

Minor errors may be corrected quietly if they do not affect meaning. Meaningful errors require transparent correction. Critical errors may require takedown or emergency communication.

Published error handling must be calm, fast, and honest.

## Maintenance Review

Post-publication QA feeds maintenance. Assets may need refresh, update, repackage, recaption, relink, or retirement. Maintenance triggers should be archived.

High-value evergreen assets should receive scheduled QA review even without reported errors.

Maintenance is part of quality governance, not optional cleanup.

# PART 23 - QA Knowledge Memory

## Purpose of QA Memory

QA Knowledge Memory preserves the studio's accumulated quality decisions. It records precedents, recurring risks, approved interpretations, rejected phrasings, visual cautions, safety lessons, correction history, and reviewer guidance.

Without QA memory, future agents repeat past mistakes. With QA memory, the studio becomes more consistent and careful over time.

QA memory must be curated. It should not become a pile of comments. It should become usable governance intelligence.

## Precedent Records

Precedent records document decisions that should influence future review. A precedent may concern karma language, depiction of hell realms, handling of grief, Pure Land framing, visual treatment of the Buddha, title boundaries, or community moderation.

Each precedent must include context, decision, reasoning, affected standards, date, and whether it is binding or advisory.

Precedents prevent inconsistent decisions across reviewers.

## Rejected Language Bank

The Rejected Language Bank stores phrases and patterns that QA has found unsafe, inaccurate, manipulative, or off-brand. It should include the reason for rejection and safer replacement principles.

Rejected language may include fear-based karma phrases, guaranteed merit claims, false healing promises, guru-like certainty, sensational hooks, and blame-oriented family language.

The bank should teach principles, not only ban words. A banned phrase can return in new wording if the underlying pattern is not understood.

## Visual Caution Library

The Visual Caution Library records visual patterns that failed or required special handling. It may include AI artifact examples, sacred-image errors, inaccurate robes, disrespectful crops, unreadable typography, inappropriate hell realm imagery, or misleading thumbnail compositions.

The library should include explanation and corrective principles. Future visual agents and reviewers should learn from it before generating or approving assets.

Visual memory protects dignity at scale.

## Safety Precedent Library

The Safety Precedent Library records how the studio handled grief, suicide-related comments, abuse disclosures, illness, trauma, children, family conflict, and crisis language. It must be handled with privacy and care.

The library should avoid storing unnecessary personal details. It should capture patterns and approved responses.

Safety precedents must be reviewed periodically to ensure they remain aligned with current standards.

## Quality Pattern Reports

Quality Pattern Reports summarize recurring QA findings over a defined period. They identify top issue categories, severity trends, affected engines, root causes, and recommended improvements.

These reports should inform training, templates, checklists, and governance updates.

Pattern reports help leadership see quality as a system.

## Memory Promotion and Retirement

QA memory items may be promoted from observation to guideline to standard. Promotion requires evidence, review, and governance approval. Not every QA note becomes a permanent rule.

Memory items may also be retired when outdated, superseded, or no longer relevant. Retired items should remain archived with reason and date.

Curated memory keeps QA useful and prevents fossilized overconstraint.

# PART 24 - QA Operating Cadence

## Purpose of Operating Cadence

QA needs rhythm. Without cadence, review becomes reactive and deadline-driven. Operating cadence defines when QA work happens, how it is planned, and how quality governance remains visible across the studio.

Cadence must include daily or per-asset checks, weekly production review, monthly pattern review, quarterly governance review, and annual standards review where appropriate.

The rhythm should support calm quality, not bureaucracy for its own sake.

## Per-Asset QA

Per-asset QA occurs whenever an artifact moves toward public use. It follows the QA workflow and applies the required domain gates.

Per-asset QA must be scheduled into production timelines. It should not be treated as a final-minute obstacle.

The production calendar must include time for revision and re-review.

## Weekly QA Review

Weekly QA review examines active assets, blockers, high-risk items, revision queues, emergency holds, and upcoming publishing deadlines. It ensures that quality issues are visible before they become crises.

The weekly review should include Lead QA, production coordination, and relevant domain owners.

The focus is operational clarity: what is blocked, why, who owns it, and what happens next.

## Monthly Quality Pattern Review

Monthly review examines issue patterns, recurring failures, quality debt, reviewer load, and engine feedback. It should identify systemic improvements.

This review should produce specific actions such as template updates, training notes, checklist changes, source guidance, or workflow adjustment.

Monthly review prevents the same errors from being solved one asset at a time forever.

## Quarterly Governance Review

Quarterly review evaluates whether QA standards remain aligned with the governing documents and whether drift is occurring. It should sample published assets, active series, growth packaging, visual outputs, community surfaces, and archive records.

The review should ask whether the channel still feels truthful, compassionate, dignified, and consistent.

Quarterly governance review protects long-term reputation.

## Annual Standards Review

Annual standards review considers whether new research, platform changes, AI capabilities, accessibility expectations, or production scale require updates to QA templates, checklists, or governing recommendations.

Annual review must not lower standards for convenience. It may improve clarity, efficiency, and coverage.

The annual review should produce a standards update log.

# PART 25 - Final QA Constitution

## Constitutional Role

The QA Engine functions as the studio's constitution for quality. It constrains power, protects mission, and ensures that no engine can publish solely because it is productive, beautiful, persuasive, or popular.

The constitution is not anti-creation. It makes creation trustworthy. It allows the studio to scale without losing moral seriousness.

Every future engine must recognize QA authority.

## Non-Negotiable Rules

No fabricated scripture.

No unsupported doctrinal certainty.

No false historical claims.

No fear-based growth.

No spiritual superiority.

No medical or mental health overreach.

No crisis mishandling.

No disrespectful sacred imagery.

No inaccessible public asset when accessibility correction is feasible.

No wrong-version publication.

No unarchived approval.

No high-risk AI self-approval.

No public asset without traceability.

No metric-based override of compassion.

No deadline-based override of truth.

## Final Review Question

Before approval, QA must ask:

**Can this asset be released with a clear conscience, defended with evidence, understood by its intended audience, received safely by vulnerable viewers, maintained by future teams, and remembered by the studio without regret?**

If the answer is no, the asset is not ready.

If the answer is uncertain, the asset requires revision or escalation.

If the answer is yes, the audit trail must show why.
