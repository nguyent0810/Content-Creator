## Document Role

This Character Bible is the source of truth for **Diêm La Vương (King Yama)** as he appears within Kinh Địa Tạng Bồ Tát Bổn Nguyện, specifically Chapter 8 ("The Praises of King Yama and His Followers" / "Diêm La Vương Chúng Tán Thán"). It exists primarily as a **disambiguation and safety document**: Diêm La Vương is the figure most commonly and most damagingly confused with Địa Tạng Bồ Tát himself, and is also frequently and incorrectly merged with a second, unrelated figure from the same chapter, the ghost king Chủ Mạng. This document exists to prevent both errors before they reach a script.

This document is not a script, not a devotional text, and not an invitation to build an episode centered on Diêm La Vương as a protagonist. Diêm La Vương is a supporting, subordinate figure whose narrative function in this sutra is to question and praise Ksitigarbha, not to act independently or to judge with personal authority.

# Character Identity

## Canonical Name

**Diêm La Vương**

This is the working Vietnamese name for channel use. The chapter title itself uses the plural form ("các Vua Diêm La" / "the kings of Yama and their followers"), signaling that this is not necessarily a single individual but a class/office of figures with attendant ghost kings. Scripts should default to "Diêm La Vương" (singular, most recognizable) for clarity but may note the plural framing when explaining the chapter's structure.

## Alternative Names

- Diêm Vương (common short form, folk usage)
- Diêm La Thiên Tử ("Yama the Heavenly Son" - a form emphasizing his status as a deva-like figure rather than a purely demonic one)
- Vua Diêm La / các Vua Diêm La (plural, matches the chapter's own Han title)
- Yama Raja / King Yama / Lord Yama / Emperor Yama (English)
- Yanluo Wang, 阎罗王 (Chinese, the source of the Vietnamese "Diêm La Vương")

Use alternative names only when clarity requires it. Default to "Diêm La Vương" in narration.

## Chinese

阎罗王 (Yanluo Wang). Confidence: high for the name as widely used in Chinese Buddhist and folk-religious contexts. The "Thập Điện Diêm Vương" (Ten Kings of Hell, Yama traditionally the fifth of ten) is a later Chinese folk-religious cosmology layered on top of the Buddhist figure and must be labeled separately - see Historical & Canonical Status below.

## Sanskrit / Pali

Yama (Sanskrit and Pali both use this form; the figure has pre-Buddhist Vedic origins later absorbed into Buddhist cosmology). Confidence: high for the name's existence across Buddhist textual traditions generally; medium for any specific doctrinal claim about Yama's exact role, since this varies significantly between Pali Canon material, Mahayana sutras, and East Asian folk reception.

## Vietnamese

Canonical form: **Diêm La Vương**. Common devotional/folk form: **Diêm Vương**. Editorial note: the honorific "Vương" (king) is standard here and, unlike its use with Địa Tạng, does correctly denote something closer to worldly/administrative authority - but even so, this authority is explicitly framed in the sutra as functioning within karmic law, not as independent, arbitrary power (see Core Teachings).

## English

Preferred working form: **King Yama**. Acceptable: Yama, Lord Yama. Avoid: "the Grim Reaper," "the death god," "Judge of the Dead" as a standalone unqualified title, or any framing implying Yama personally and arbitrarily decides who lives, dies, or suffers.

## Honorific Titles

- King Yama, praiser and questioner of Ksitigarbha Bodhisattva (Chapter 8's own narrative role for him).
- A guardian/administrator figure within the karmic order, not a sovereign above it.

## Common Nicknames

- Diêm Vương (folk short form). Formal narration should default back to "Diêm La Vương."

## Common Misidentifications

Diêm La Vương must not be misidentified as:

- **Địa Tạng Bồ Tát.** This is the single most important and most common error for this entire series. Diêm La Vương and Địa Tạng Bồ Tát are two entirely different figures with entirely different narrative functions: Diêm La Vương questions, praises, and is hierarchically subordinate to Ksitigarbha in Chapter 8; Ksitigarbha is the bodhisattva of vast vow and compassion who is the sutra's central figure. CB_BUD_001 already documents this as "Misconception 2" and lists it under Common Misidentifications and the Knowledge Graph ("Địa Tạng Bồ Tát --must not be confused with--> Diêm La Vương"). This document exists in part to reinforce that boundary from the other direction.
- **The ghost king Chủ Mạng**, who also appears in Chapter 8. This is a second, separate, and specifically documented error risk (see below): the Buddha confirms in the sutra that Chủ Mạng - not Diêm La Vương - is the compassionate manifestation of a great bodhisattva. Diêm La Vương himself receives no such confirmation in this sutra. Conflating the two figures, or attributing Chủ Mạng's bodhisattva-manifestation status to Diêm La Vương, is a documented risk for this chapter. See the Continuity Registry (`DOMAINS/BUDDHISM/CONTINUITY_REGISTRY.md`) for the full correction record.
- A Western "Grim Reaper" or death-god archetype who personally decides who dies and when. Buddhist cosmology (per both Pali-tradition and Mahayana framing) presents Yama as operating within, not above, the law of karma.
- The sole or ultimate judge of any being's afterlife. Chapter 8 explicitly shows Yama in a posture of kneeling, questioning, and praising - not commanding, sentencing, or ruling independently.
- A singular unique individual in all contexts - the chapter's own title uses the plural ("kings of Yama"), and later Chinese folk cosmology developed a system of Ten Kings, of which Yama is traditionally counted as one (see below); do not present "Diêm La Vương" as necessarily meaning one and only one being across all Buddhist and folk-religious contexts without labeling which layer of tradition is being discussed.

# Historical & Canonical Status

## Historical Confidence

- **High:** Yama is a widely recognized figure across Buddhist textual and folk-religious traditions in South, Southeast, and East Asia, including within Kinh Địa Tạng Bồ Tát Bổn Nguyện, Chapter 8.
- **High:** within this sutra specifically, Yama's narrative function is to question Ksitigarbha (why do rescued beings relapse into wrongdoing?) and to praise his power, not to act as an independent authority.
- **Medium:** the precise doctrinal relationship between Pali Canon presentations of Yama (where he is generally described as operating within, not above, karmic law) and East Asian Mahayana/folk presentations requires source-specific care; do not assume they are identical.
- **Low unless sourced and explicitly labeled as folk tradition:** the "Thập Điện Diêm Vương" (Ten Kings of Hell) system, in which Yama is traditionally the fifth of ten kings each presiding over a stage of judgment, is a Chinese folk-religious cosmological development that arose later and separately from the core Buddhist sutra material. It should never be presented as content of Kinh Địa Tạng Bồ Tát Bổn Nguyện itself. If referenced, it must be explicitly labeled "Chinese folk-religious tradition," distinct from "canonical content of this sutra."

## Canonical Appearance

Primary canonical appearance for this channel: **Kinh Địa Tạng Bồ Tát Bổn Nguyện, Chapter 8 ("Diêm La Vương Chúng Tán Thán" / "The Praises of King Yama and His Followers")**. Diêm La Vương appears alongside countless ghost kings, arriving from within the Great Iron Ring mountains at the Trayastrimsa assembly to hear the Buddha teach.

Do not import material about Yama from other Buddhist sutras, from the Pali Canon, or from Chinese folk-religious Ten Kings literature into a script about this sutra's Chapter 8 without explicitly labeling the source and tradition.

## Legend vs Canon

- **Canonical scripture (this sutra):** Diêm La Vương's appearance, question, and praise of Ksitigarbha in Chapter 8.
- **Broader Buddhist tradition (other texts):** Yama's role in Pali Canon material and other Buddhist cosmological texts as an administrator of karmic law.
- **Folk/legendary tradition:** the Ten Kings of Hell system, popular iconography of Yama as a fearsome judge with a court and ledger, and various regional folk stories. Must be labeled explicitly as folk tradition, separate from this sutra, if used at all.
- **Modern interpretation:** Yama as a symbol of conscience, moral seriousness, or the psychological experience of reckoning with one's actions - permissible as clearly labeled modern reflection, not as literal doctrine.

# Role in Kinh Địa Tạng Bồ Tát Bổn Nguyện

## Narrative Function in Chapter 8

Diêm La Vương arrives with countless ghost kings from the Iron Ring mountains to the Trayastrimsa assembly. The Buddha invites the assembly to speak freely. After the ghost king "Evil Poison" (Ác Độc) explains the ghost kings' protective self-understanding, Diêm La Vương kneels and praises Ksitigarbha's inconceivable authority and wisdom in tirelessly rescuing beings across the six paths for countless kalpas. He then raises the chapter's central question: if Ksitigarbha's rescues are so powerful, why do beings so often fall back into wrongdoing and evil paths soon after being helped? The Buddha answers with the parable of a person shown the way off a dangerous road who, their mind still confused, wanders back onto it anyway - locating the cause of relapse in the being's own persisting habits and karma, not in any deficiency of Ksitigarbha's power.

This positions Diêm La Vương, within this sutra, as a **respectful questioner and devotee**, not an adversary, not a peer in status to Ksitigarbha, and not an independent authority handing down sentences. His posture throughout is kneeling, questioning, and praising.

## Relationship to Ksitigarbha Bodhisattva

The relationship documented in this chapter is one of **hierarchical reverence, not rivalry or equivalence**: Diêm La Vương and his retinue rely on the power of the Buddha and Ksitigarbha simply to reach the assembly; Diêm La Vương's question is framed with humility and genuine puzzlement, not challenge; and the ghost kings collectively vow to protect anyone who performs even a small wholesome act, treating such devotion "as if guarding the Buddha himself." There is no conflict, contest, or adversarial framing between Diêm La Vương and Ksitigarbha anywhere in this chapter.

## Relationship to Chủ Mạng and the Other Ghost Kings

Diêm La Vương is one figure among many who arrive together in Chapter 8 - alongside roughly two dozen named ghost kings, including Ác Độc (who speaks first) and Chủ Mạng (who tracks conception, birth, and lifelong conduct, and whom the Buddha explicitly confirms to be a bodhisattva's compassionate manifestation, destined for future Buddhahood as "Vô Tướng Như Lai"). **Diêm La Vương himself does not receive this confirmation in the sutra.** Any script referencing "the bodhisattva hidden in Chapter 8" must refer to Chủ Mạng, not Diêm La Vương. See CB_BUD_003's sibling document coverage is not applicable here (Chủ Mạng does not yet have a dedicated character bible; treat this distinction as mandatory whenever both figures are discussed together) and see the Continuity Registry for the full correction record.

## Underworld Administration as Karmic Bookkeeping

Across both Pali-tradition framing and this sutra's own presentation, Yama's function is best understood as a role within a self-operating system of karmic cause and effect, not as a personal, independent power to reward or condemn. Ghost kings under his general association describe their work as observing and responding to beings' own actions, not authoring outcomes from personal will. This is the single most important doctrinal point to preserve when depicting Diêm La Vương: **karma is not administered by Yama's preference; Yama's role (as this sutra frames the broader ghost-king office) is closer to a witness and administrator operating within a law that already governs outcomes.**

# Character Psychology (Narrative Role, Not Invented Personality)

Because Diêm La Vương is a scriptural figure appearing briefly in one chapter, do not invent an elaborate personal psychology, backstory, or inner conflict for him. What the text supports:

- **Posture:** humble, kneeling, questioning - not commanding or superior.
- **Motivation (as shown):** genuine puzzlement about why rescued beings relapse, and reverence for Ksitigarbha's power and patience.
- **Tone:** solemn and respectful, consistent with the whole chapter's tone.

Do not write fictional dialogue for Diêm La Vương beyond a paraphrase of his documented question and praise. Do not give him a villain arc, an internal moral struggle, or a redemption arc - none of this is supported by the source and it would import an adversarial framing the text does not contain.

# Symbols and Visual Identity

## Visual Rules

- Diêm La Vương may be depicted with traditional East Asian judge/king iconography (robes, seated posture, an implied administrative or record-keeping role) but should be shown in a posture of **kneeling or bowing toward the Buddha and Ksitigarbha** when depicted within a Chapter 8 scene, consistent with the text.
- Facial expression: solemn, attentive, respectful - not menacing, not gleeful, not cruel.
- Avoid a "court of judgment" visual that centers Diêm La Vương as the presiding authority over a trembling defendant; this imports Ten Kings folk iconography and creator-judge framing this packet must avoid (see KP_BUD_001 Hell Realm Rule and Chapter 8 cautions).
- Lighting and palette should follow the series' existing restrained, non-horror palette (earth tones, muted gold, temple red accents, warm lamplight) rather than a green/purple "underworld" horror palette.

## Forbidden Depictions

- Diêm La Vương as equal in status or stature to Địa Tạng Bồ Tát in any composition.
- Diêm La Vương as a Grim Reaper figure (scythe, skeletal form, black hooded cloak) - this imports an unrelated Western death-god visual tradition.
- Diêm La Vương depicted torturing, whipping, or personally harming beings.
- Diêm La Vương as a horror-genre antagonist, jump-scare figure, or comedic bureaucrat caricature - both extremes undermine the chapter's actual solemn, respectful tone.
- Diêm La Vương's court depicted as an arbitrary tribunal where his personal judgment - rather than the being's own karma - determines the outcome.

## Objects and Setting

Approved: robes, seated or kneeling posture, an implied ledger/record motif (consistent with the "administrator of karmic bookkeeping" framing), the Iron Ring mountain setting as backdrop, the Trayastrimsa assembly as the scene's context.

Avoid inventing objects (scythes, hourglasses, judgment scales as a dominant visual metaphor) not supported by the sutra or by documented East Asian Buddhist iconography for this figure.

# Common Misconceptions

## Misconception 1: Diêm La Vương is the same figure as Địa Tạng Bồ Tát.

Correct understanding: they are two entirely different figures. Địa Tạng Bồ Tát is the sutra's central bodhisattva of vow and compassion. Diêm La Vương is a supporting figure who questions and praises him in Chapter 8. See CB_BUD_001 Misconception 2.

## Misconception 2: Diêm La Vương is confirmed in this sutra to be a bodhisattva's manifestation.

Correct understanding: that confirmation belongs to a different figure in the same chapter, the ghost king Chủ Mạng, not to Diêm La Vương. This is a documented, high-priority correction - see the Continuity Registry.

## Misconception 3: Diêm La Vương personally and arbitrarily decides who suffers and who is saved.

Correct understanding: the sutra and broader Buddhist doctrine present Yama's role as operating within the law of karma, not above or outside it - closer to an administrator/witness than an independent judge with personal discretion.

## Misconception 4: Diêm La Vương is a single, unique individual identical across all Buddhist and folk traditions.

Correct understanding: the chapter's own title is plural ("kings of Yama"), and later Chinese folk religion developed a separate Ten Kings system. These layers must be labeled distinctly, not flattened into one figure.

## Misconception 5: Diêm La Vương is an adversary or rival of Ksitigarbha.

Correct understanding: the chapter shows no conflict between them. Diêm La Vương's posture is reverent and subordinate throughout.

# FAQ

1. **Who is Diêm La Vương in Kinh Địa Tạng?** A figure who arrives with countless ghost kings in Chapter 8, kneels, praises Ksitigarbha's power, and asks why rescued beings relapse into wrongdoing.
2. **Is Diêm La Vương the same as Địa Tạng Bồ Tát?** No. They are entirely different figures with entirely different roles.
3. **Is Diêm La Vương a bodhisattva in disguise, like Chủ Mạng?** No. The sutra confirms this specifically for Chủ Mạng, not for Diêm La Vương. Do not conflate the two.
4. **Does Diêm La Vương judge people?** He functions within karmic law rather than as an independent judge with personal discretion; do not portray him as a creator-god-style ultimate judge.
5. **Is there more than one Diêm La Vương?** The chapter's title itself is plural ("kings of Yama"); later Chinese folk tradition developed a separate system of Ten Kings, of which Yama is traditionally one - label this as folk tradition if referenced.
6. **Can Diêm La Vương be shown as a Grim Reaper?** No. This imports an unrelated Western visual tradition and is a forbidden depiction.
7. **What is Diêm La Vương's tone in this chapter?** Solemn, humble, respectful - kneeling and questioning, not commanding.
8. **Can Diêm La Vương be portrayed as an adversary of Ksitigarbha?** No. The text shows no conflict; his posture is reverent throughout.
9. **What should a script do if it needs to mention both Diêm La Vương and Chủ Mạng?** Explicitly name them as two different figures and state clearly that only Chủ Mạng is confirmed as a bodhisattva's manifestation.
10. **Where should a writer check before using this character?** This document, CB_BUD_001 (for the Địa Tạng distinction), KP_BUD_001 Chapter 8 knowledge map, and the Continuity Registry.
